## Biography & Context
**Thorkild Peter Rudolph Jacobsen** (1904–1993) was a Danish-born American historian, philologist, and one of the twentieth century's foremost Assyriologists and scholars of Sumerian literature, serving as Director of the Oriental Institute at the University of Chicago and later Professor of Assyriology at Harvard University. In his groundbreaking July 1943 article *"Primitive Democracy in Ancient Mesopotamia"* (*Journal of Near Eastern Studies* 3/2, pp. 159–172)—examined directly in `ARTICLES-REPORTS/Democracy/HW2-1--Democracy-in-Mesopotamia.pdf` and expanded in [[the-secret-history-of-democracy-primitive-democracy]]—Jacobsen overturned the prevailing view that ancient Mesopotamia knew only theocratic autocracy.

Through close reading of Sumerian and Akkadian cuneiform texts, including *Gilgamesh and Agga*, the *Epic of Gilgamesh*, *Enuma Elish*, and Old Assyrian merchant archives from Kanesh (Kültepe in Anatolia), Jacobsen demonstrated that third-millennium BC Mesopotamian city-states were governed by a bicameral general assembly (*ukkin* / *puhrum*) of elders (*abba*) and free arms-bearing citizens (*guruš*). In this system, kings such as **Gilgamesh of Uruk** could not initiate war or peace without first securing the assembly's deliberative consent, and even the divine pantheon under Anu and Enlil was modeled as a parliamentary assembly governed by persuasion (*"asking one another"*) rather than unilateral edict (source: [[the-secret-history-of-democracy-primitive-democracy]], pp. 1–2, 21–25).
