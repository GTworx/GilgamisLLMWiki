## Overview
This interdisciplinary neurological and historical treatise revisits Julian Jaynes' provocative 1976 thesis on the breakdown of the bicameral mind. The authors examine the neuroanatomy of auditory verbal hallucinations, right-hemisphere linguistic capacity, and historical textual evidence from the Iliad and ancient Mesopotamian literature suggesting that subjective introspective consciousness emerged only around 1200–800 BCE.

## Key Takeaways
- **Key Finding**: Synthesizes Jaynesian bicameralism: ancient humans experienced volition as auditory hallucinations commandingly heard as gods.
- **Key Finding**: Connects bicameral breakdown to the Bronze Age Collapse, mass literacy, and the necessary emergence of introspective deception.
- **Key Finding**: Examines modern neuroimaging of schizophrenia and right-hemisphere temporal lobe activation during inner speech.

## Critical Excerpts & Analysis
> "Before human beings possessed an introspective mind-space of their own, volition was experienced as external auditory commands attributed to ancestors, kings, and gods."
