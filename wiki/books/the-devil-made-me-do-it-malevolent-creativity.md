## Executive Summary

Authored by University of South Australia researchers **R. M. J. McBain**, **David H. Cropley**, and **Phillip S. Kavanagh** (51-page `.docx` research manuscript, pp. 1–3, regression tables pp. 49–51), *The Devil Made Me Do It* investigates **malevolent creativity**—the deliberate generation of novel, effective ideas intended to inflict harm (ranging from white-collar fraud to asymmetric terrorist innovations like 9/11, p. 2).

Addressing two major gaps in the "dark side of creativity" literature—causal experimental design and the neglected role of environmental **Press** (situational climate/priming) relative to the other 3 Ps (*Person, Product, Process*)—the authors use experimental priming and hypothetical moral scenarios alongside Big Five and Dark Triad personality measures (Extraversion, Openness, Narcissism, Psychopathy, pp. 49–51) to test what actually triggers harmful creative choices.

---

## Key Empirical Findings

1. **Situational Press Outweighs Fixed Personality**: While subclinical psychopathy and narcissism correlate with selecting malevolent options in regression models (pp. 49–51), experimental **Press** (environmental cues and situational framing) emerged as surprisingly powerful in shifting both perception and choice of malevolent vs. benevolent creative solutions (pp. 1–2).
2. **Implications for Security & Organizational Ethics**: Suggests that preventing malevolent innovation—whether corporate misconduct, cybercrime, or extremist tactics—depends less on hunting static "evil personalities" than on dismantling toxic situational climates and incentive structures that prime harmful novelty.
