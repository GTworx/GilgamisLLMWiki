## Summary
Compendium of landmark 2025 paleobiological and paleoanthropological discoveries, including the osteology of a new sail-backed iguanodontian dinosaur from the Wealden Group, the hand biomechanics of Paranthropus boisei revealing precision tool-making grip, the geochemical impact of intermittent lead exposure on hominid brain evolution, and the phylogenetic evolution of primate kissing.

## Analytical Overview
This volume gathers four major paleobiological and evolutionary anthropology investigations published in 2025, shedding new light on vertebrate anatomical transitions and the selective pressures shaping hominin evolution. The first investigation, led by Jeremy Lockwood and colleagues, provides an exhaustive osteological description of a newly discovered sail-backed styracosternan dinosaur from the Lower Cretaceous Wealden Group of the Isle of Wight, England. Analyzing its hyper-elongated dorsal neural spines, the authors evaluate biomechanical and physiological hypotheses, demonstrating that these dorsal structures functioned primarily as socio-sexual display structures and species recognition crests rather than thermoregulatory sails.

In paleoanthropology, Carrie S. Mongle and team present fossil hand bones of *Paranthropus boisei* from Koobi Fora, Kenya. Long characterized as an evolutionary dead-end specialized exclusively in megadont chewing of coarse vegetation, these exquisite fossil elements demonstrate that *P. boisei* possessed a human-like precision grip with an opposable thumb and robust apical tufts. This biomechanical evidence proves that *Paranthropus* had the morphological capacity to manufacture and manipulate stone tools, challenging the longstanding paradigm that tool culture was the exclusive domain of the genus *Homo*.

Complementing this physical evidence, Renaud Joannes-Boyau and collaborators employ laser ablation mass spectrometry on hominid teeth from South Africa, revealing that early hominins experienced intermittent, naturally occurring environmental lead exposure during enamel formation. This chronic toxic insult likely imposed severe neurodevelopmental stress, influencing neural wiring, cognitive plasticity, and life history evolution. Finally, Matilda Brindle presents a phylogenetic comparative analysis of kissing behavior across primates, demonstrating that mouth-to-mouth tactile contact evolved from maternal premastication and grooming into an adaptive mechanism for assessment of biological compatibility and chemical signaling.
