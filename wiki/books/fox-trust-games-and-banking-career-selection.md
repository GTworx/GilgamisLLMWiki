## Summary
Investigates economic experiments at German universities demonstrating that students who score lowest on trustworthiness in behavioral trust games are disproportionately likely to pursue careers in finance.

## Analytical Overview
### Behavioral Economics of the Financial Personality

In *They Failed a Trust Test, Then Chose Finance*, Bloomberg Opinion columnist Justin Fox analyzes a fascinating and controversial empirical study conducted by German economists examining student career selection. For decades, sociologists debated whether investment banking and high finance actively corrupts good people through misaligned compensation incentives, or whether financial institutions systematically attract self-interested, low-trust individuals from the outset.

### The Trust Game Experiment and Self-Selection

The researchers administered classic Berg-Dickhaut-McCabe **Trust Games** to undergraduate students across multiple disciplines:
- In the game, players decide how much money to send to an anonymous partner (measuring trust) and the partner decides how much to return (measuring trustworthiness).
- The study tracked students longitudinally: **those who exhibited the lowest levels of trustworthiness and reciprocity were statistically far more likely to select finance and investment banking majors** over non-profit, engineering, or public sector careers.

### Structural Implications for Financial Regulation

Fox discusses why this self-selection effect is profound: financial institutions cannot rely on voluntary ethical codes or executive goodwill. Without rigorous external regulatory constraints and enforceable clawback penalties, financial markets will inevitably suffer from endemic moral hazard.
