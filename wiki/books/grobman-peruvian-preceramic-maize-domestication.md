## Executive Summary
Published in *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* (PNAS) by an international team of agronomists and archaeologists led by Alexander Grobman and Tom Dillehay, this landmark 8-page paper documents the earliest directly dated macrobotanical maize remains from coastal South America. Excavations at Paredones and Huaca Prieta in northern Peru yielded well-preserved fossilized corn cobs, husks, stalks, and tassels radiocarbon-dated to 6,700–3,000 cal BP. Morphological and phytolith analyses prove that early farmers were cultivating primitive popcorn and flour corn varieties (Proto-Confite Morocho) millennia earlier than previously established, illustrating how Mesoamerican teosinte descendants were rapidly adapted to diverse Andean coastal microclimates.

---

## Core Thematic Insights & Structural Framework

- **Early Holocene Andean Maize Chronology**: Directly dates complete fossilized maize cobs to at least 6,700 cal BP, pushing back the chronology of South American agricultural intensification.
- **Primitive Popcorn Morphology**: The macrobotanical remains exhibit tiny cobs with 8–10 rows of small, hard kernels, diagnostic of primitive ancestral popcorn varieties popped over direct embers.
- **Trans-Continental Agricultural Diffusion**: Proves that early cultivated Zea mays dispersed rapidly from its domestication center in southwestern Mexico across Central America into the arid Peruvian coast.
- **Culinary and Ritual Significance**: Shows that early maize was initially consumed as popped snacks and fermented ritual chicha beer centuries before becoming an everyday caloric staple.

---

## Key Data Points & Excerpts

> "The macrobotanical corn specimens from Huaca Prieta provide incontrovertible evidence that primitive maize was integrated into Peruvian preceramic coastal diets by the sixth millennium BP."

> "Maize domestication was not a singular event, but a continuous multi-millennial evolutionary dialogue between human selective harvesting and plant genetic plasticity."
