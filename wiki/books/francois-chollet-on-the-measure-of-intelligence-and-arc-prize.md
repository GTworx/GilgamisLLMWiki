## Summary
Pioneering theoretical treatise on the definition and measurement of intelligence in biological and artificial systems. Features François Chollet's foundational 64-page paper 'On the Measure of Intelligence', which rigorously defines intelligence as skill-acquisition efficiency over broad novel task distributions, introducing the Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus (ARC) benchmark to expose the fundamental limits of brute-force interpolation.

## Analytical Overview
This volume provides a foundational philosophical and mathematical critique of contemporary AI evaluation paradigms.

Key theoretical breakthroughs:
1. **Defining Intelligence as Generalization Efficiency**: Chollet formally rejects task-specific performance (e.g., chess, Go, standardized exams) as a measure of intelligence, demonstrating that high performance can be achieved through brute-force memorization of training priors. Instead, intelligence is mathematically formalized as **skill-acquisition efficiency**—the speed and sample efficiency with which an agent converts informational experience into novel operational skill over previously unseen task distributions.
2. **The Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus (ARC)**: Details the design of the ARC benchmark, which requires agents to solve visual grid transformation puzzles relying only on core human knowledge priors (objectness, goal-directedness, basic geometry, counting) without any domain-specific training data, effectively measuring true out-of-distribution reasoning.
3. **Interpolation vs. Synthesis**: Contrasts deep learning's reliance on geometric interpolation along dense manifold clusters with human-like discrete program synthesis and inductive logic.
