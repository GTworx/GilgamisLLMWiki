## Executive Summary

Published in 1865 by British economist and logician **William Stanley Jevons** (here in the revised 1866 2nd edition, pp. 1–4), *The Coal Question* is one of the earliest and most prophetic works of resource and energy economics. Opening with an epigraph from Adam Smith on the vitality of the "progressive state" (p. 5), Jevons challenged Victorian complacency by arguing that Britain's industrial supremacy rested on a finite, non-renewable geological endowment—cheap, accessible coal—whose rising extraction costs from ever-deeper seams would eventually erode Britain's global advantage relative to resource-rich rivals like the United States (pp. 2, 29–47).

Its most enduring theoretical legacy is **Chapter VII ("Of the Economy of Fuel")**, which introduced the celebrated **Jevons Paradox**: far from reducing total coal consumption, technological improvements that make steam engines and furnaces *more fuel-efficient* lower the effective cost of energy, accelerate industrial expansion, and thereby *increase* aggregate resource consumption.

---

## Core Analytical Pillars (Contents, p. 4)

- **Geological & Mining Cost Limits (Chapters III–V, pp. 29–47)**: Shows that exhaustion is an *economic* threshold rather than literal depletion—Britain loses competitiveness the moment mining depth drives coal prices above foreign competitors.
- **The Jevons Paradox (Chapter VII — *Of the Economy of Fuel*)**: Demonstrates empirically across Scottish blast furnaces and Watt steam engines that efficiency gains stimulate wider adoption and higher total throughput—a principle still central to modern AI compute demand and clean-energy rebound debates.
- **Substitutes, Population & Fiscal Policy (Chapters VIII–XVIII)**: Evaluates alternative energy sources available in 1865, maps exponential industrial growth curves, and urges Britain to pay down its national debt while coal-driven prosperity lasts.
