## Executive Summary

Edited by Robert Arnot for M. Walter Dunne's *Universal Classics Library* (1903, pp. 2–5), *The Sufistic Quatrains of Omar Khayyam* is a comprehensive comparative compendium assembling the three most influential 19th-century European interpretive lenses on the Nishapur astronomer-poet within a single volume: **Edward FitzGerald's** iconic poetic transcreation (1st edition plus variant tables across editions 2–5 and Edward Heron-Allen's quatrain-by-quatrain source analysis), **E. H. Whinfield's** scholarly metrical translation of 500 quatrains, and a prose English rendering of French consul **J. B. Nicolas's** 464-quatrain edition (pp. 4–6).

By juxtaposing FitzGerald's agnostic-Epicurean Omar, Whinfield's nuanced philosophical skeptic, and Nicolas's thoroughly mystical Sufi allegorist—framed by Arnot's General Introduction on Omar's place in Sufism (pp. 6–7)—the anthology turns the *Rubaiyat* into an extraordinary case study in how translation reshapes philosophical meaning.

## Structural Overview (Table of Contents, p. 6)

1. **General Introduction by Robert Arnot** (pp. xi–xxii): Reviews early Islamic biographical witnesses starting with Shahrazuri's *Nuzhat al-Arwah* (identifying Omar as successor to Avicenna/Ibn Sina in philosophy and renowned for prodigious memory, p. 7) and situates Khayyam's wine-and-beloved lexicon within Persian Sufi symbolism.
2. **Edward FitzGerald's First Edition & Apparatus** (pp. 1–128): Reprints FitzGerald's 1859 introduction, the complete 75-stanza first edition (including the *Kuza-Nama* "Book of Pots"), **Edward Heron-Allen's** exhaustive source-tracking analysis of FitzGerald's 101-stanza 5th edition (pp. 35–114), and complete textual variation tables across FitzGerald's first four editions (pp. 115–128).
3. **E. H. Whinfield's 500 Quatrains** (pp. 129–266): Translates a wide manuscript selection in rhymed English quatrains, showcasing Omar's full tonal spectrum—satire of hypocrites, astronomical fatalism, tender melancholy, mystical longing, and tavern defiance.
4. **J. B. Nicolas's 464 Quatrains** (pp. 267–309): Translates into English prose the 1867 French edition by Nicolas, who famously contended that Khayyam's "wine," "cupbearer," and "tavern" should be read consistently as veiled Sufi allegories for divine love and spiritual intoxication.

## Key Comparative Insights

1. **Three Omars in One Book**: Reading the same Persian motifs side by side reveals the central hermeneutic divide in Khayyam studies—is Omar an anti-clerical materialist celebrating fleeting sensory joy (FitzGerald), a deeply conflicted scientist oscillating between doubt and piety (Whinfield), or an esoteric Sufi mystic speaking in coded symbols (Nicolas)?
2. **Forensic Mapping of FitzGerald's Craft**: Heron-Allen's included analysis demonstrates that while nearly half of FitzGerald's stanzas translate identifiable quatrains from the Bodleian and Calcutta manuscripts, others are composite mosaics blending two or more quatrains or borrowing imagery from Attar and Hafez.
3. **The Polymath Behind the Poet**: Arnot's historical survey reminds readers that in his own century Omar Khayyam was revered first as a master mathematician, algebraist, and calendar reformer (*Jalali* calendar) rather than merely a bohemian poet.
