## Summary
Dual synthesis bridging advanced statistical methodology in food sensory discrimination (triangle test replicate modeling) with macroscopic global nutritional epidemiology, quantifying the structural divergence between national domestic food production profiles and human nutritional dietary requirements across 185 nations.

## Analytical Overview
This volume unites two vital poles of food science and political agronomy: the micro-level statistical rigor required for organoleptic food product testing, and the macro-level systemic modeling of global agricultural output against planetary dietary guidelines. The first treatise, authored by statistician J. Kunert and colleagues, provides a definitive mathematical resolution to a longstanding controversy in sensory evaluation: how to properly analyze replicated triangle tests without violating independence assumptions.

Through beta-binomial distributions, generalized estimating equations (GEE), and mixed-effects generalized linear models, the authors prove that standard binomial calculations severely underestimate Type I error rates when panellists perform repeated evaluations. They formulate robust variance-correction protocols that enable food developers, sensory scientists, and quality assurance labs to accurately measure subtle differences in reformulated recipes, alternative protein substitutes, and low-sodium matrixes.

The second treatise, published in Nature Food (2025), deploys global production and trade datasets across 185 countries to model the 'nutritional production gap'—the quantitative mismatch between what nations domestically produce and what their populations require according to evidence-based dietary guidelines (EAT-Lancet planetary health diet). The findings reveal severe global structural imbalances: while high-income agricultural exporters massively overproduce refined grains, sugar, and livestock feed, over 70% of developing nations suffer chronic production deficits in nutrient-dense fruits, vegetables, legumes, and nuts. The work demonstrates that closing this health and ecological gap requires fundamentally repurposing agricultural subsidies away from feed grains and biofuels toward localized, nutrient-dense horticultural systems.
