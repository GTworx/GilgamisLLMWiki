## Executive Summary

Written by Wright State University philosophy professor **William B. Irvine** (Oxford University Press, 2009, p. 2), *A Guide to the Good Life* sparked the twenty-first-century revival of practical Stoicism among modern readers, executives, and psychologists. Lamenting that contemporary academic philosophy abandoned its ancient Greek vocation of teaching *"a philosophy of life"* (Introduction & Part One, pp. 3–7), Irvine reconstructs Roman Stoicism—focusing on Seneca, Musonius Rufus, Epictetus, and Marcus Aurelius—as an empirically grounded psychological toolkit aimed at lasting **tranquility (*ataraxia*)** and deep existential joy rather than joyless grimness.

Across four accessible parts (*The Rise of Stoicism*, *Stoic Psychological Techniques*, *Stoic Advice*, and *Stoicism for Modern Lives*, TOC pp. 3–6), Irvine adapts ancient exercises directly to contemporary anxieties: hedonic adaptation, status anxiety, social insults, grief, anger, aging, and mortality.

## Core Modernized Stoic Techniques (Part Two)

1. **Negative Visualization (*Premeditatio Malorum*, Ch. 4)**: Periodically imagining the loss of loved ones, health, or possessions reverses **hedonic adaptation**, flooding ordinary life with vivid gratitude while inoculating the mind against sudden shock.
2. **The Trichotomy of Control (Ch. 5)**: Refines Epictetus's classic Dichotomy into three categories—things over which we have *complete control* (values, internal goals), *no control* (the past, weather), and *partial control* (winning a match)—urging practitioners to internalize goals inside the third category (e.g., aiming to play one's best rather than to win).
3. **Fatalism Toward the Past & Present (Ch. 6)**: Practices radical acceptance of what has already occurred while preserving energetic agency toward the future.
4. **Voluntary Discomfort (Ch. 7)**: Deliberately choosing mild hardship (cold exposure, fasting, simple fare) builds confidence, diminishes fear of ruin, and sharpens appreciation for simple comforts.
