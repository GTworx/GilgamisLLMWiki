## Executive Summary

*After the Prophet: The Epic Story of the Shia-Sunni Split in Islam* (2009, Doubleday, p. 329) is journalist and religious historian **Lesley Hazleton's** gripping narrative history of the turbulent fifty-year succession crisis following the death of the Prophet Muhammad in 632 CE—a political and moral fracture that permanently divided the Islamic world into its **Sunni** and **Shia** branches.

Structured as a three-act historical tragedy centered on **Muhammad** (Part One), his cousin and son-in-law **Ali ibn Abi Talib** (Part Two), and his grandson **Hussein** at Karbala in 680 CE (Part Three, TOC p. 4), Hazleton draws on classical Arabic chronicles (notably al-Tabari) and modern scholarship to portray the protagonists not as flat theological icons, but as flesh-and-blood political actors—including the formidable **Aisha** and **Fatima**, pragmatic statesman **Abu Bakr**, stern **Umar**, dynastic tactician **Muawiya**, and principled yet tragic **Ali** and **Hussein**.

---

## Three-Act Narrative Architecture (Contents, TOC)

1. **Part One — Muhammad (Chapters 1–5, pp. 11–98)**: Opens at Muhammad's deathbed in Medina (632 CE), examining the disputed final moments, the hasty assembly at Saqifa that elected **Abu Bakr** as first Caliph while **Ali** and **Fatima** tended to the Prophet's burial, and the deep psychological fault line created between consultative seniority (the Sunni precedent) and prophetic lineage/charismatic designation (the Shia claim of Ghadir Khumm).
2. **Part Two — Ali (Chapters 6–11, pp. 99–220)**: Traces the expansion of the early Caliphate under Umar and Uthman, the assassination of Uthman that propelled Ali into a contested caliphate, the traumatic first civil war (*Fitna*)—including the **Battle of the Camel** against Aisha and the stalemate at **Siffin** against Umayyad governor **Muawiya**—and Ali's assassination in Kufa by Kharijite extremists in 661 CE.
3. **Part Three — Hussein (Chapters 12–15, pp. 221–299)**: Chronicles the transformation of the Caliphate into hereditary Umayyad monarchy under Muawiya and his son **Yazid**, culminating in the 680 CE massacre of **Hussein** and seventy-two companions on the arid plains of **Karbala**—an event that crystallized Shia identity around redemptive martyrdom, Ashura remembrance, and moral defiance against tyrannical power.

## Key Historical & Geopolitical Insights

1. **Political Origin of a Theological Divide**: Hazleton demonstrates that the Sunni–Shia split began not over scripture or creed, but over a classic political-constitutional question: *who legitimately rules a community after its charismatic founder dies?*
2. **The Moral Power of Defeat**: While the Umayyads won the battlefield at Karbala, Hussein's refusal to pledge allegiance to Yazid turned military annihilation into an enduring spiritual and political archetype of resistance that still shapes Middle Eastern geopolitics fourteen centuries later.
3. **Women at the Center of Early Islamic Politics**: Gives sustained historical weight to **Fatima**, **Aisha**, and **Zaynab** as decisive voices in shaping communal memory and succession politics.
