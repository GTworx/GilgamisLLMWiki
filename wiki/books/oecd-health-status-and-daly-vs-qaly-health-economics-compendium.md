## Executive Summary
This 5-page two-part health economics and policy compendium combines the OECD Health Status Executive Summary (2023) with a methodological comparison of Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALY) versus Quality-Adjusted Life Years (QALY).

The OECD report provides cross-national benchmarks on life expectancy, preventable mortality, healthcare expenditure per capita, and chronic disease prevalence across advanced industrialized economies. The companion health economics brief provides an analysis comparing DALYs (a measure of burden developed by the WHO and Global Burden of Disease measuring years of life lost due to premature mortality and years lived with disability) and QALYs (an economic evaluation metric measuring the health-related quality of life gained from therapeutic interventions).

---

## Core Thematic Insights & Structural Framework

- **OECD Comparative Health Benchmarks**: Analyzes life expectancy recovery post-pandemic, noting persistent health spending escalation approaching 10% to 17% of GDP across OECD member nations.
- **DALY Methodological Mechanics**: Details the calculation of DALYs as $YLL + YLD$, where Years of Life Lost ($YLL$) and Years Lived with Disability ($YLD$) quantify societal burden from zero (perfect health) to one (death).
- **QALY Economic Utility**: Details QALYs as a measure where one QALY equals one year of life in perfect health, used predominantly in cost-utility analyses by agencies such as the UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE).
- **Ethical Controversies in Value Metrics**: Explores the ethical debate surrounding disability weighting, age weighting, and whether QALY-based decision thresholds discriminate against individuals with pre-existing permanent disabilities.

## Key Data Points & Excerpts

> "Health economics requires rigorous units of measurement: DALYs quantify the burden of human suffering that disease inflicts, while QALYs quantify the value of life restored by clinical intervention."

> "Comparing healthcare systems through standardized health metrics reveals that higher per-capita spending does not automatically translate into longer or healthier human lives."
