## Executive Summary

Written by eminent Collège de France Roman historian **Paul Veyne** (English edition 2003, Routledge, tr. David Sullivan, pp. 2–6), *Seneca: The Life of a Stoic* is a brilliant, unsentimental intellectual biography wrestling with history's most famous philosophical paradox: how **Seneca**—one of Rome's wealthiest statesmen, tutor and chief minister to the tyrannical Emperor **Nero**—simultaneously authored some of antiquity's purest treatises on Stoic virtue, voluntary simplicity, and calm death.

Structured across three sweeping chapters—*Prologue: Birth to Final Disillusion (1–65 CE)*, *Seneca as a Stoic*, and *Epilogue: Final Witness and Death of the Liberator (63–65 CE)* (p. 6)—Veyne strips away both naive hero-worship and glib accusations of hypocrisy, showing Stoicism as an intense spiritual discipline for navigating a brutal imperial autocracy where retirement itself could invite execution.

---

## Key Historical & Philosophical Insights

1. **Stoicism as Imperial Survival & Dignity**: Contextualizes why Roman senators embraced Stoicism under Caligula, Claudius, and Nero—not as passive fatalism, but as an internal armor preserving agency under arbitrary despotism.
2. **Moral Realism vs. Perfectionism**: Seneca openly acknowledged he was not a perfected "Sage" (*sapiens*), only an imperfect traveler striving daily toward self-command—making his final forced suicide in 65 CE a deliberate philosophical witness.
