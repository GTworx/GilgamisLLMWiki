## Executive Summary

Edward Heron-Allen's 1898 edition of *The Ruba'iyat of Omar Khayyam* is a landmark of Victorian Orientalist textual scholarship and comparative philology. Published in a collector's limited print run by H. S. Nichols Ltd. in London (pp. 6, 11–12), it presents the complete photographic facsimile of **Ouseley MS. 140** in the Bodleian Library at Oxford—dated A.H. 865 (1460 CE), the oldest manuscript of Khayyam's quatrains then known in Europe and the primary source used by Edward FitzGerald—paired with a modern Persian typographic transcript, a strict literal English prose translation, exhaustive textual notes, and an extensive Khayyam bibliography (p. 15).

Rather than competing with FitzGerald's free poetic paraphrase, Heron-Allen's mission is forensic recovery: stripping away Victorian romantic embroidery to reveal exactly what the 15th-century Persian codex actually says, quatrain by quatrain.

## Key Philological & Critical Contributions

1. **Demystifying the "Three Schoolfellows" Legend**: In his Introduction (p. 17), Heron-Allen critically interrogates the oft-repeated story linking Omar Khayyam, Seljuk vizier Nizam al-Mulk, and Assassin founder Hasan-i Sabbah, weighing early Persian chroniclers against chronological evidence.
2. **Literal Fidelity vs. Poetic Transcreation**: By anchoring every English phrase to the transcribed Persian manuscript, Heron-Allen enabled subsequent scholars—including his own later comparative analysis printed in [[the-sufistic-quatrains-of-omar-khayyam]]—to measure precisely how much of FitzGerald's celebrated poem derives directly from Khayyam versus creative synthesis.
3. **Manuscript Transparency**: Including the complete photographic facsimile allows independent paleographic verification of the 158-quatrain Bodleian baseline, establishing a benchmark against bloated later manuscripts containing over a thousand attributed quatrains.
