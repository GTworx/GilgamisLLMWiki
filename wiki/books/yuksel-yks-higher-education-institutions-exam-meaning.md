## Summary
Investigates the psychological, existential, and socio-economic burden of the Turkish Central University Entrance Examination (YKS) through phenomenological student qualitative research.

## Analytical Overview
### The High-Stakes Crucible of Turkish Adolescence

In *What Does the Higher Education Institutions Exam (YKS) Mean for Students?*, published in the *Journal of Higher Education and Science*, Ahmet Yüksel, Zeynep Eşit Çelik, and Serdar Erkan examine the psychological, social, and existential realities experienced by millions of Turkish youth confronting the centralized **Yükseköğretim Kurumları Sınavı (YKS)**. With over three million applicants competing for a limited quota of viable professional university slots, the exam operates as a massive societal bottleneck.

### Phenomenological Metaphors of the Exam

Through qualitative phenomenological interviews with secondary school graduates, the authors uncover the visceral metaphors students use to conceptualize the exam:
- Students describe the exam as a 'life-or-death battle', a 'modern lottery', an 'inescapable prison', and a 'guillotine' that collapses years of personal identity into a single three-hour multiple-choice score.
- The exam induces intense psychosomatic disorders, familial conflict, depression, and severe existential paralysis.

### The Educational and Social Cost of Exam-Centric Schooling

The study exposes how centralized high-stakes testing hollows out genuine secondary education: classrooms devolve into test-taking drill mills, genuine curiosity is extinguished, and non-tested humanities, arts, and creative sciences are completely marginalized.
