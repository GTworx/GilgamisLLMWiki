## Executive Summary

In *David and Goliath* (2013, Little, Brown, pp. 239–241), **Malcolm Gladwell** flips conventional assumptions about power and weakness—beginning with the biblical duel itself, where David was never an unarmed weakling but an asymmetric projectile slinger facing a slow, heavily armored, possibly acromegalic (vision-impaired) giant expecting close-quarters infantry combat.

Across three parts—**The Advantages of Disadvantages**, **The Theory of Desirable Difficulty**, and **The Limits of Power**—Gladwell explores **inverted-U curves** (showing how smaller school classes, elite university prestige, or overwhelming coercive force actually backfire past an optimal point), **Big Fish–Little Pond** psychology, compensatory resilience born from dyslexia or trauma, and why harsh coercive overreaction erodes state legitimacy (from Northern Ireland's Troubles to Birmingham's civil rights campaign and Le Chambon's rescue of Jewish refugees).
