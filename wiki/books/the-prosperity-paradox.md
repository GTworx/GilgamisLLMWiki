## Executive Summary

Written by **Clayton M. Christensen**, Christensen Institute Global Prosperity Practice lead **Efosa Ojomo**, and former *HBR* editor **Karen Dillon** (copyright p. 281; About the Authors p. 278), *The Prosperity Paradox* applies Disruptive Innovation theory to one of economics' thorniest riddles: why trillions of dollars in foreign aid, top-down infrastructure projects, and institutional-reform mandates have failed to lift dozens of developing nations out of poverty, whereas countries like the United States (1850s), Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan transformed rapidly (Preface pp. 6–7; Section 2).

The authors resolve the paradox by distinguishing three fundamentally different types of innovation—**Sustaining**, **Efficiency**, and **Market-Creating** (Ch. 2, pp. 27–47)—arguing that only **Market-Creating Innovations** (turning non-consumers into paying consumers via simple, affordable products) generate the self-sustaining jobs, local supply chains, tax revenues, and grassroots demand that *pull* durable infrastructure and honest institutions into existence (Ch. 4, 8–10).

---

## Key Analytical Insights

1. **Three Innovation Archetypes (Ch. 2)**: **Sustaining innovations** improve existing products for current customers; **efficiency innovations** cut costs and often eliminate jobs (e.g., Mexico's maquiladora trap, Ch. 7); only **market-creating innovations** (Ford's Model T, Sony's transistor radio, Celtel's African mobile networks, Indomie noodles in Nigeria) unlock vast non-consumption.
2. **Pull vs. Push Development (Ch. 4, 10)**: Top-down "push" development—building clinics, roads, or schools without local economic engines to maintain them—routinely decays; market-creating ventures create a "pull" that finances and sustains roads, power grids, rule of law, and education organically.
3. **Rethinking Corruption & Institutions (Ch. 8–9)**: Provocatively frames everyday corruption in poor economies not merely as moral failure, but as a workaround ("a solution") people hire to navigate broken scarcity systems—meaning anti-corruption campaigns succeed only alongside market-creating prosperity that gives citizens an economic stake in transparent institutions.
