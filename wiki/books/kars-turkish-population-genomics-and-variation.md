## Summary
Landmark high-coverage whole-genome sequencing study mapping the population genetics, deep historical admixture, and rare variant architecture of modern Turkey across 16 administrative sub-regions, establishing an indispensable genomic reference catalog for Anatolian medicine and Eurasian human migrations.

## Analytical Overview
This canonical genomic treatise represents the first comprehensive, high-coverage whole-genome sequencing (WGS) atlas of the contemporary Turkish population. Published by Mehmet E. Kars and the Turkish Population Genomics Consortium, the study sequences over 3,300 individuals across all geographical regions of Turkey, capturing over 10 million novel single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) and small insertions/deletions (indels) that were absent from global databases such as 1000 Genomes and gnomAD.

Through principal component analysis (PCA), ADMIXTURE clustering, and f-statistics, the researchers uncover the intricate genomic palimpsest of Anatolia—a historic geographic bridge connecting the Middle East, the Caucasus, Europe, and Central Asia. The data demonstrate that while the modern Turkish genetic pool clusters closest to Southern European and Mediterranean populations (particularly Greek, Italian, and Iberian cohorts) and Caucasus populations, it carries an unmistakable signature of medieval Central Asian Turkic migration (ranging from 9% to 22% East Eurasian/Turkic admixture across distinct provinces), confirming historical linguistic and cultural transitions without total genetic replacement.

From a clinical perspective, the volume provides critical insights into the medical consequences of regional endogamy and consanguinity in Turkey, where first-cousin marriages historically elevated the homozygous expression of ultra-rare deleterious recessive variants. By establishing an authoritative national allele frequency reference database, this work directly empowers genetic diagnostics, prenatal screening, and targeted therapeutics for rare metabolic, neurological, and cardiovascular disorders across Turkish and broader Middle Eastern populations.
