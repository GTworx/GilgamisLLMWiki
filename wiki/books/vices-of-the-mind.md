## Executive Summary

Written by University of Warwick philosopher **Quassim Cassam** (Oxford University Press, 2019, copyright p. 5) in the wake of the political upheavals of 2016 and explicitly inspired by Susan Stebbing's 1939 classic *Thinking to Some Purpose* (Preface p. 8), *Vices of the Mind* pioneers **vice epistemology**—the systematic study of the character traits, attitudes, and thinking styles that systematically obstruct acquiring, retaining, and sharing knowledge.

Examining high-stakes historical and political disasters—including the 2003 Iraq WMD intelligence failure, the 1973 Yom Kippur surprise, climate denial, Brexit polarization, and conspiracy theorizing—Cassam develops **Obstructivism**: epistemic vices (such as **closed-mindedness**, **intellectual arrogance**, **dogmatism**, **epistemic insouciance**, **wishful thinking**, and **gullibility**) are blameworthy not merely because they offend abstract ideals, but because they actively degrade democratic judgment and produce catastrophic real-world harm (Ch. 1–6).

---

## Core Concepts in Vice Epistemology

1. **Character Vices, Attitudes & Thinking Styles (Ch. 1–4)**: Broadens epistemology beyond static personality traits to include **epistemic postures** (like *epistemic insouciance*—casual indifference to whether one's claims are true) and habitual *vicious thinking* (e.g., conspiratorial reasoning).
2. **Stealthy Vices (Ch. 7, pp. 159–181)**: Explains why intellectual arrogance and closed-mindedness are uniquely dangerous: they are *stealthy*, actively concealing their own presence from the very person afflicted by them.
3. **Responsibility & Self-Improvement (Ch. 6, 8)**: Distinguishes acquisition responsibility from revision responsibility, arguing that although we cannot always cure our blind spots through solitary introspection, institutional checks, adversarial critique, and intellectual humility can mitigate epistemic vice.
