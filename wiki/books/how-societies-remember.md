## Executive Summary

Written by Cambridge social theorist **Paul Connerton** (1940–2019) in the *Themes in the Social Sciences* series (Cambridge University Press, 1989, copyright & Contents p. 2)—our library scan preserves the complete front matter, Introduction, and foundational **Chapter 1: Social Memory** across 21 two-page spreads—*How Societies Remember* is a foundational classic of collective-memory studies. Building on Maurice Halbwachs's insight that individual recollection always operates within social frameworks, Connerton asks a sharp sociological question: *how are shared images of the past actually conveyed and sustained across generations?*

Challenging hermeneutic scholarship's obsessive focus on written archives (**inscribing practices**), Connerton argues that societies preserve collective identity just as powerfully through non-textual, habitual **incorporating practices**—specifically **commemorative ceremonies** (Ch. 2) and **bodily practices** such as posture, gesture, table manners, and ritual dress (Ch. 3).

---

## Key Theoretical Distinctions

1. **Three Classes of Memory (Ch. 1)**: Differentiates **personal memory** (autobiographical recall), **cognitive memory** (facts, rules, language), and **habit-memory** (embodied capacity to reproduce a performance without explicit recollection)—showing how social theorists neglected habit-memory's political power.
2. **Revolutionary Rupture & Historical Reconstruction**: Analyzes moments like the French Revolution's regicide of Louis XVI and calendar overhaul to show how regimes attempt to engineer collective forgetting—and why embodied social habits stubbornly outlast formal decrees.
3. **Inscribing vs. Incorporating Transmission**: Demonstrates that ritual performance and bodily etiquette embed political authority and group identity directly into muscle memory, making them far harder to question than written texts.
