## Executive Summary

Written by George Washington University ancient historian and archaeologist **Eric H. Cline** (Princeton University Press, 2014; consolidated here from the complete 265-pp. monograph and the 13-pp. Prologue extract), *1177 B.C.* reconstructs the sudden, catastrophic collapse of the interconnected Late Bronze Age Mediterranean and Near Eastern world system. Opening with Pharaoh Ramses III's Medinet Habu inscriptions recording the onslaught of the enigmatic **"Sea Peoples"**—identified in Egyptian records as distinct confederated groups (*Peleset, Tjekker, Shekelesh, Shardana, Danuna, Weshesh*, Prologue p. 1)—Cline traces how the glittering "Club of Great Powers" (**Egypt, Hatti [the Hittites], Mycenae, Mitanni, Assyria, Babylonia, Cyprus [Alashiya], and Canaanite Ugarit**) unraveled within a single generation.

Drawing on the Amarna Letters, the Uluburun shipwreck, and Ugarit's last kiln-baked distress tablets, Cline rejects monocausal explanations (invasions alone, earthquakes alone, or drought alone) in favor of **Complexity Theory and Systems Collapse**: a tightly coupled, hyper-specialized international trade network (dependent on long-distance Afghan tin and Cypriot copper for bronze) fell victim to a cascading **"perfect storm"** of megadrought, famine, seismic storms, internal rebellions, severed trade routes, and displaced Sea Peoples.

## Core Historical & Analytical Frameworks

1. **The First Globalized World System (15th–13th Centuries BCE)**: Documents royal gift exchange, diplomatic marriages, international merchant networks, and commodity interdependence linking Mycenaean Greece, Minoan Crete, Hittite Anatolia, Egypt, and Mesopotamia.
2. **Systems Collapse & Cascading Failure**: Demonstrates how hyper-connected civilizations lose resilience when simultaneous environmental, economic, and military shocks overwhelm centralized palace bureaucracies—providing a sobering historical analog for twenty-first-century globalization.
3. **Creative Destruction into the Iron Age**: Shows how the destruction of Bronze Age palace empires cleared space for decentralized Iron Age innovations—iron metallurgy, the Phoenician alphabet, Israelite/Aramaean polities, and archaic Greek city-states.
