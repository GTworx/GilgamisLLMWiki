## Executive Summary

First published in German in 1900 (*Philosophie des Geldes*) and presented here in David Frisby's authoritative 2004 Routledge third enlarged edition (pp. 3–4), **Georg Simmel's** *The Philosophy of Money* is a sociological and philosophical masterwork examining how the spread of the mature money economy reshapes human subjectivity, personal freedom, social trust, urban rhythm, and cultural meaning.

Rather than treating money strictly through technical monetary theory, Simmel constructs a two-part inquiry—an **Analytical Part** (Value and Money, Substance vs. Function, Money in the Sequence of Purposes) and a **Synthetic Part** (Individual Freedom, the Money Equivalent of Personal Values, and the Style of Life, p. 5)—showing how money acts simultaneously as the ultimate tool of individual emancipation from feudal dependency *and* an impersonal solvent that flattens qualitative uniqueness into interchangeable quantitative price.

## Key Philosophical & Sociological Insights

1. **Relational Theory of Value**: Economic value is neither purely intrinsic to objects nor mere subjective whim; it emerges from the *distance* and sacrifice overcome through social exchange (Chapter 1, p. 5).
2. **Double-Edged Emancipation**: Replacing personal feudal dues with cash payments liberates individuals from personal servitude, allowing anonymity and wider social circles—yet simultaneously replaces warm local solidarity with abstract, calculative detachment.
3. **The Blase Attitude & Culture of Speed**: Money compresses time and space, accelerating the tempo of modern metropolitan life while breeding emotional numbness (*Blasiertheit*) as every qualitative experience is reduced to a common numerical denominator.
4. **Objective vs. Subjective Culture**: Anticipating modern critiques of information overload, Simmel warns that humanity's accumulated technological and institutional machinery ("objective culture") expands far faster than any individual soul's capacity to assimilate it ("subjective culture").
