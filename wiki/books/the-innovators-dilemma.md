## Executive Summary

Harvard Business School professor **Clayton M. Christensen**'s landmark 1997 classic—part of the *Management of Innovation and Change* series (pp. 2–3)—solved one of strategy's deepest puzzles: why extraordinarily well-managed market leaders repeatedly collapse in the face of technological shifts even while listening closely to their best customers and investing aggressively in R&D. Grounded in meticulous empirical histories of the **hard disk drive industry** (14-inch down to 1.8-inch drives, Ch. 1, pp. 36–71), **hydraulic mechanical excavators** (Ch. 3, pp. 121–142), and steel minimills (Ch. 4), Christensen proves that **good management itself**—allocating capital to high-margin mainstream customers—blinds incumbents to **disruptive technologies**.

Whereas incumbents almost always win **sustaining innovations** (even radical ones that improve performance along metrics mainstream buyers already value), **disruptive technologies** arrive cheaper, simpler, smaller, and initially *worse* on mainstream metrics. Because technology improves faster than market demand can absorb it, those initially inferior upstarts climb the performance trajectory until they overtake the incumbent's core market from below (Ch. 1–4, 9).

## Five Principles of Disruptive Innovation (Part Two, pp. 166–335)

1. **Resource Dependence**: Companies depend on current customers and investors for resources; listening to top accounts systematically starves low-margin disruptive bets (Ch. 5).
2. **Small Markets Don't Solve Large Companies' Growth Needs**: A $4 billion incumbent cannot move its growth needle in an emerging $10 million niche, forcing executives to wait until markets are "large enough"—by which time upstarts already dominate (Ch. 6).
3. **Markets That Don't Exist Cannot Be Analyzed**: Applying conventional market-forecasting rigor to disruptive markets produces paralysis; firms must adopt **discovery-driven planning** designed for learning and rapid iteration (Ch. 7).
4. **Resources, Processes, and Values (RPV)**: An organization's capabilities also define its disabilities; incumbents can rarely incubate disruptive models inside legacy cost structures and must spin up **autonomous organizations** whose size matches the emerging market (Ch. 5–6, 8).
5. **Technology Supply vs. Market Demand**: When sustaining innovation overshoots mainstream needs, competitive advantage shifts from raw performance to reliability, convenience, and price—opening the door for disruptive entrants (Ch. 9).
