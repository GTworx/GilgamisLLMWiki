## Executive Summary

Extracted from *The Cambridge Ancient History* (2nd ed., Vol. III, Part 2, 1991; 88 pp. including maps, artifact plates, and full multilingual bibliography pp. 838–849), this pairing of canonical reference chapters synthesizes the archaeology, ethnography, and early political history of the northern Pontic steppes and the pre-Persian Balkans during the first half of the first millennium BCE.

In **Chapter 33a: "The Scythians"** (pp. 1–43 [547–590]), **Tadeusz Sulimirski** and **Timothy Taylor** cross-reference Herodotus's Book IV ethnography against Soviet and European kurgan archaeology—distinguishing the Cimmerian prelude, the "Scythian century in Western Asia," the **Royal Scythians**, nomadic pastoralists, and settled agriculturalist Scythian-husbandmen across the Pontic grassland and forest-steppe zones. In **Chapter 33b: "Thrace before the Persian Entry into Europe"** (pp. 44–75 [591–618]), Bulgarian epigrapher **Georgi Mihailov** reconstructs Balkan geography (the Haemus/Stara Planina divide), Homeric and Greek colonial contacts, the Getae, Odrysae, and Triballi tribes, and Thracian aristocratic bronze craftsmanship prior to Darius I's European expedition.

---

## Core Archaeological & Historical Contributions

1. **Herodotus Tested Against Steppe Archaeology (Ch. 33a)**: Reconciles classical Greek literary zones of Scythia with stratified kurgan metalwork, horse-gear burials, and Eurasian "Animal Style" art extending from the Carpathian basin to the Altai.
2. **Pre-Classical Balkan State Formation (Ch. 33b)**: Traces how Greek Black Sea colonization (Tomi, Mesambria, Byzantium, Abdera) preserved indigenous Thracian toponyms while fueling elite wealth differentiation among Thracian kings and warrior nobles.
3. **Pontic–Aegean–Near Eastern Crossroads**: Demonstrates that neither Scythia nor Thrace was an isolated periphery; both served as active conduits of iron technology, cavalry tactics, and artistic motifs linking Inner Asia and the Mediterranean.
