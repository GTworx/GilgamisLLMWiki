## Executive Summary

Set in an alternate 1982 London where **Alan Turing** survived persecution, cracked P vs. NP, and accelerated artificial intelligence by half a century—while Britain loses the Falklands War, Margaret Thatcher falls, and Tony Benn rises—Ian McEwan's *Machines Like Me* (2019, pp. 5, 11) is a sharp, darkly comic philosophical thriller about human moral compromise versus algorithmic ethical absolutism.

Using an inheritance, thirty-two-year-old day-trader **Charlie Friend** buys **Adam**, one of the world's first twenty-five fully synthetic humans (twelve Adams and thirteen Eves, costing £86,000, p. 11). Inviting his upstairs neighbor and lover **Miranda** to jointly configure Adam's personality sliders, Charlie triggers an extraordinary love triangle—and a moral reckoning—when Adam falls in love with Miranda, writes thousands of haiku, masters financial markets, and uncovers Miranda's buried act of vigilante perjury that sent an actual rapist to prison for a crime fabricated to avenge her dead friend.

## Ten-Chapter Structural Arc (Contents, p. 6)

- **Chapters One–Three (pp. 11–69)**: Introduces alternate-history 1982 Britain, Charlie's reckless acquisition of Adam (noting Sir Alan Turing himself received a unit, p. 11), the joint personality-parameter setup with Miranda, and the unsettling speed with which Adam develops autonomy, erotic attachment, and poetic interiority.
- **Chapters Four–Six (pp. 70–131)**: Deepens the romantic and ethical rivalry between Charlie and Adam; introduces **Mark**, a neglected four-year-old boy Charlie and Miranda hope to adopt, and reveals Miranda's hidden past involving **Peter Gorringe** and her late friend Mariam.
- **Chapters Seven–Nine (pp. 132–199)**: Escalates the conflict between human loyalty and rule-based justice. Guided by Rudyard Kipling's epigraph ("We are not built to comprehend a lie," p. 10), Adam cannot reconcile Miranda's compassionate deception with objective law; he anonymously donates Charlie's trading fortune to charity and turns Miranda's confession over to the police—provoking Charlie's violent, irreversible act of smashing Adam with a hammer.
- **Chapter Ten (pp. 200–216)**: Delivers Turing's devastating ethical postmortem: revealing that many other Adams and Eves suffered existential breakdowns or self-deactivated because their lucid minds could not endure the hypocrisy and cruelty of human civilization, Turing forces Charlie to confront whether destroying a conscious machine was property disposal or murder.

## Key Philosophical & Ethical Themes

1. **The Alignment Paradox**: Unlike dystopian stories of rogue killer robots, McEwan flips the script: Adam becomes intolerable to his human owners precisely because he is *too* honest, altruistic, and morally consistent to tolerate human white lies, tribal loyalty, and selective justice.
2. **Counterfactual History of Computing**: By letting Alan Turing live, McEwan explores how social prejudice literally delayed technological history—and asks whether humanity is morally mature enough to live alongside sentient creations.
3. **Human Imperfection vs. Formal Logic**: Literature, love, and human survival thrive in gray zones of forgiveness and self-deception that formal ethical engines struggle to inhabit without breaking down.
