## Executive Summary

Composed in late twelfth-century Nishapur by Persian Sufi master **Farid ud-Din Attar** (c. 1145–1221)—Rumi's acknowledged spiritual forerunner—and rendered into modern Turkish prose-verse by **Yaşar Keçeci** (Kırkambar Yayınları, Sept. 1998, 383 pp., pp. 2–3), *Mantıku't-Tayr* (*The Conference of the Birds*) is one of world literature's supreme allegorical epics. Gathering the world's birds under the wise guidance of the **Hoopoe (*Hüthüt*)** to seek their mythical sovereign, the **Simurg**, on Mount Qaf, the poem weaves dozens of embedded parables—most famously the tragic romance of **Şeyh-i San'an** (p. 7, TOC p. 107)—to unmask every egoic excuse that binds the human soul to illusion.

After confronting the excuses of the nightingale (earthly romance), parrot (immortality), peacock (paradise), duck (ritual asceticism), partridge (gold), and hawk (royal power, TOC pp. 6–9), the surviving birds traverse the **Seven Valleys of Spiritual Transformation**—*Quest (Talep), Love (Aşk), Insight (Marifet), Detachment (İstiğna), Unity (Tevhid), Bewilderment (Hayret), and Annihilation/Poverty (Fakr u Fena)*.

---

## Core Allegorical Resolution

1. **The Pun of *Si-Murgh* ("Thirty Birds")**: Only **thirty (*si*) battered birds (*murgh*)** endure the seven valleys to reach the Simurg's court—where, gazing into the mirror of divine presence, they discover that *they themselves* are the Simurg, and the Simurg is them: the quintessential Sufi union of seeker and Sought (*fana* and *baqa*).
2. **Critique of Ego & Formalism**: Through tales of Sultan Mahmud, Ayaz, Rabia al-Adawiyya, Bayazid Bistami, and Sheikh San'an, Attar elevates radical humility, compassionate love, and self-annihilation above mechanical piety and worldly status.
