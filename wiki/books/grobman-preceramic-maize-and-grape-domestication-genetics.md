## Summary
Pioneering archaeobotanical and paleogenomic synthesis exploring the multi-millennial evolutionary trajectory of agricultural flora, documenting preceramic maize macrofossils at Paredones and Huaca Prieta in Peru dating back over 6,700 years, alongside genomic analyses of dual grape domestication across western Eurasia and etymological botanical inquiries into floral heliotropism.

## Analytical Overview
This canonical work synthesizes breakthrough empirical investigations into prehistoric crop domestication, botanical genomics, and cultural archaeobotany. Drawing upon landmark archaeological excavations at Paredones and Huaca Prieta along the arid northern coast of Peru led by Alexander Grobman, Duccio Bonavia, and Tom Dillehay, the research analyzes exceptionally preserved fossil maize cobs, husks, stalks, and tassels directly dated via accelerator mass spectrometry (AMS) to between 6,700 and 3,000 calibrated years before present (cal BP). These specimens demonstrate that prehistoric South American coastal foragers and emergent agriculturalists were already cultivating multiple morphologically distinct races of maize—including proto-confite morocho, proto-kculli, and ancestral popcorn variants—far earlier than previously recognized, proving that maize morphological diversification accelerated dramatically following its initial dispersal from southwestern Mexico.

Complementing the New World maize records, the work incorporates global paleogenomic sequencing of Vitis vinifera across western Eurasia. Genomic analysis of thousands of cultivated grapevine accessions and wild sylvestris relatives establishes a dual domestication model: one independent lineage originating in the South Caucasus (~11,000 years ago coinciding with early Neolithic agriculture) and another in the Levant, driven by convergent phenotypic selection for berry size, hermaphroditism, sugar accumulation, and vegetative propagation resilience. Furthermore, the volume explores cultural and etymological botanical trajectories, analyzing why Helianthus annuus is etymologically designated as 'ayçiçeği' (moon flower) rather than sun flower in Turkish lexical structures, illustrating the intersections of folk taxonomy, phenotypic observation, and agricultural linguistic history.
