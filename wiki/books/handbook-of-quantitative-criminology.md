## Executive Summary

*Handbook of Quantitative Criminology* (published by Springer, 2010, 787 pp., `DOI: 10.1007/978-0-387-77650-7`) edited by Professors [[alex-r-piquero]] (Florida State University) and [[david-weisburd]] (Hebrew University of Jerusalem / George Mason University), with a Foreword by Alfred Blumstein, is the landmark 35-chapter reference work codifying the statistical, spatial, longitudinal, and experimental methodologies of modern criminology and criminal justice policy (source: BOOKS/Law/2010_Book_HandbookOfQuantitativeCriminol.pdf, pp. 3–9, 13–21). Tracing the discipline's evolution from qualitative case studies and raw FBI Uniform Crime Reports (1929) to high-dimensional econometric and randomized experimental science, the volume demonstrates how quantitative methods uncover the spatial clustering of crime, developmental life-course offending trajectories, and the causal efficacy of policing and correctional interventions (source: BOOKS/Law/2010_Book_HandbookOfQuantitativeCriminol.pdf, pp. 5–6, 13–18).

Structured across three major parts, the Handbook covers **Innovative Descriptive & Spatial Methods** (GIS crime mapping by Jerry Ratcliffe, exploratory data visualization by Michael Maltz, Daniel Nagin's group-based trajectory modeling, social network analysis of co-offending, and systematic social observation), **Experimental & Quasi-Experimental Causal Inference** (place-randomized hot-spots trials by Robert Boruch, [[david-weisburd]], and Richard Berk; longitudinal-experimental studies by David Farrington and Rolf Loeber; propensity score matching; instrumental variables; and regression discontinuity designs), and **Advanced Statistical Estimation** (hierarchical linear modeling, count-data Poisson/negative-binomial regression, survival/hazard models, and formal agent-based simulation) (source: BOOKS/Law/2010_Book_HandbookOfQuantitativeCriminol.pdf, pp. 7–10, 21–780).

## Key Arguments / Chapter Breakdown

* **Foreword & Part I (Chapters 1–14): Descriptive, Spatial, Developmental & Network Methods (pp. v–viii, 1–288)**:
  * *Crime Mapping, Data Visualization & Trajectory Modeling*: Details GIS spatial autocorrelation and hot-spot mapping (Ch. 2, pp. 5–24), graphical exploration of FBI Supplementary Homicide Reports (Ch. 3, pp. 25–52), Semi-Parametric Group-Based Trajectory Modeling (GBTM) distinguishing adolescent-limited from life-course-persistent offenders (Ch. 4, pp. 53–80), meta-analysis (Ch. 10), and Social Network Analysis (SNA) of gang structures (Ch. 11, pp. 209–225) (source: BOOKS/Law/2010_Book_HandbookOfQuantitativeCriminol.pdf, pp. 7–8, 21, 51).

* **Part II (Chapters 15–22): Measurement Error, Surveys & Missing Data (pp. 289–479)**:
  * *Self-Report Surveys, Victimization Panels & Imputation*: Analyzes response bias in offender self-reports and National Crime Victimization Surveys (NCVS), missing-data multiple imputation, and psychometric latent variable modeling (source: BOOKS/Law/2010_Book_HandbookOfQuantitativeCriminol.pdf, pp. 8–9, 289–479).

* **Part III (Chapters 23–35): Place-Randomized Trials, Quasi-Experiments & Hierarchical Econometrics (pp. 481–780)**:
  * *Hot-Spots Policing RCTs & Causal Identification*: Demonstrates how **Place-Randomized Trials** (Ch. 23 by Boruch, Weisburd, & Berk, pp. 481–502) prove the 'Law of Crime Concentration at Places' without merely displacing crime to adjacent blocks; pairs experimental trials with propensity score matching, regression discontinuity, spatial econometrics, and multilevel modeling (source: BOOKS/Law/2010_Book_HandbookOfQuantitativeCriminol.pdf, pp. 9–10, 481–780).

## Key Quotes & Citations

* "Quantitative methods are at the heart of social science research generally, and in criminology/criminal justice in particular. Since the discipline's birth, researchers have employed a variety of quantitative methods to describe the origins, patterning, and response to crime and criminal activity." (source: BOOKS/Law/2010_Book_HandbookOfQuantitativeCriminol.pdf, ch. 1, p. 13)
* "Largely under the initiative of Lawrence Sherman, who led with the Kansas City Preventive Patrol experiment, we have seen a striking variety of randomized social experiments testing various means of operating elements of the criminal justice system." (source: BOOKS/Law/2010_Book_HandbookOfQuantitativeCriminol.pdf, Foreword, p. 6)
