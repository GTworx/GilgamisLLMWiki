## Executive Summary

Compiled and introduced by Prof. Dr. Telman Khurshidoglu Aliyev (PDF metadata, p. 1), this 50-page anthology brings together Leo Tolstoy's late-life Russian compilation of prophetic *hadith*—originally drawn from Indian scholar **Abdullah al-Ma'mun al-Suhrawardy's** *The Sayings of Muhammad* and published by Tolstoy's popular press *Posrednik* in 1909—alongside Tolstoy's well-known 1909 correspondence with **Elena Vekilova** and excerpts from his spiritual autobiography *A Confession* (pp. 2–4, 48–50).

While the volume's framing advances a strongly apologetic claim that Tolstoy converted to Islam and that Russian/Soviet authorities suppressed his *Posrednik* booklet (pp. 1, 3–4), the underlying primary documents reveal a well-attested historical reality: in his final decade after his 1901 excommunication from the Russian Orthodox Church, Tolstoy passionately sought universal moral truths across world religions (Hinduism, Buddhism, Confucianism, and Islam) and repeatedly praised Islam's strict monotheism, ethical simplicity, and absence of clerical hierarchy compared with institutional church dogma (pp. 1, 3).

## Structural Overview (Table of Contents, pp. 2–3)

- **Publisher's Notes, Preface & Biographical Introduction** (pp. 4–12): Recounts how Tolstoy discovered al-Suhrawardy's collection of prophetic sayings and emphasizes his focus on themes of poverty, human equality, charity, and moral accountability (p. 3).
- **Chapter I — Muhammad (PBUH)**: Reproduces Tolstoy's selected aphorisms and moral maxims from al-Suhrawardy's *Sayings of Muhammad*, organized around conscience ("God's light in our hearts"), humility, justice, mercy, and universal brotherhood (p. 2).
- **Chapter II — Letters**: Presents Tolstoy's famous 1909 reply to Russian mother **Elena Vekilova** (whose sons wished to embrace Islam), in which Tolstoy affirms that anyone choosing purely between institutional ecclesiastical complexity and Islamic monotheism might understandably prefer Islam's uncluttered ethical directness, alongside notes on Tolstoy's Caucasian encounters with Naqshbandi circles (pp. 2–3).
- **Chapter III — Confessions of Tolstoy** (pp. 45–50): Excerpts Tolstoy's existential crisis and search for God from *A Confession*, reflecting on Kant's critique of rational proofs of God and the lived experiential reality of faith (pp. 48–50).

## Analytical Evaluation & Context

1. **Historical Kernel vs. Apologetic Framing**: Historians of Russian literature confirm that Tolstoy genuinely admired al-Suhrawardy's *Sayings of Muhammad*, published an abridged Russian selection via *Posrednik* (1909), and wrote warmly about Islamic monotheism in his letter to Elena Vekilova—even though mainstream scholarship treats Tolstoy as a radical universalist Christian anarchist rather than a formal convert to any institutional creed.
2. **Ethical Universalism Over Ritual Dogma**: Across both his *hadith* selections and his *Confession* excerpts, Tolstoy consistently filters religious texts for stripped-down moral imperatives: truthfulness, care for the poor, non-hypocrisy, and inner conscience.
3. **Reception History**: The booklet itself illustrates how major world authors are re-appropriated across cultural and religious borders as moral witnesses.
