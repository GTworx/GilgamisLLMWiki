## Executive Summary

*Learning by Doing: The Real Connection Between Innovation, Wages, and Wealth* (published by Yale University Press, 2015, 310 pp.) by Boston University economist and former desktop-publishing software pioneer [[james-bessen]] resolves a central paradox of modern capitalism: why major technological revolutions (both during the first 50 years of the 19th-century Industrial Revolution—*'Engels' Pause'*—and during the post-1980 Information Technology revolution) initially produce **stagnant median wages and rising inequality** for decades before eventually generating broad-based prosperity (source: BOOKS/Innovation_/2015 Learning by Doing - the real connection between_innovation, wages, and wealth.pdf, pp. 3–9, 21–36). Drawing on two centuries of quantitative economic history across 19th-century Lowell cotton textile mills, steelmaking, railroads, automated bank ATMs, and enterprise software, Bessen proves that **invention is only the beginning of innovation**—what determines whether technology enriches the few or the many is the slow, institutional accumulation and standardization of **'learning by doing'** technical skills among ordinary workers (source: BOOKS/Innovation_/2015 Learning by Doing - the real connection between_innovation, wages, and wealth.pdf, pp. 7–8, 21–136).

Bessen refutes both the Luddite myth that automation destroys aggregate employment (showing that after banks installed 400,000 ATMs, the number of human bank tellers actually *increased* because cheaper branches expanded demand and tellers shifted to relationship marketing, Ch. 7) and the naive 'Skill-Biased Technical Change' dogma that more traditional 4-year college degrees alone will cure wage inequality (Ch. 8). During the early stage of any new technology, knowledge is **uncodified, tacit, and non-standardized**, allowing only a small elite of technicians and managers to capture rents; only when industries develop **standardized interfaces**, open technical standards, employee mobility, and vocational/community-college training ecosystems do the 'skills of the unskilled' become scarce and well-paid (source: BOOKS/Innovation_/2015 Learning by Doing - the real connection between_innovation, wages, and wealth.pdf, pp. 7–8, 51–228).

## Key Arguments / Chapter Breakdown

* **Part I (Chapters 1–4): Technology as Knowledge — The Skills of the Unskilled & Revolutions in Slow Motion (pp. 9–70)**:
  * *Why Breakthrough Inventions Take Decades to Work*: Contrasts the myth of instant heroic invention (e.g., Dean Kamen's Segway vs. Wright brothers and Lowell power looms, p. 51) with empirical learning curves. Shows that early 19th-century 'unskilled' Lowell mill girls achieved massive productivity gains through uncodified **learning by doing**, and how the standardization of technical knowledge (e.g., qwerty keyboards, standardized locomotive gauges, and HTML/SQL) unlocks mass adoption (source: BOOKS/Innovation_/2015 Learning by Doing - the real connection between_innovation, wages, and wealth.pdf, pp. 7, 21–70).

* **Part II (Chapters 5–7): When Does Technology Raise Wages? — Weavers, ATMs & Scarce Skills, Not Scarce Jobs (pp. 71–136)**:
  * *Solving Engels' Pause & The ATM Paradox*: Explains why British and U.S. textile workers saw flat real wages for 40 years until power-loom skills became standardized, portable across competing mills, and costly for employers to replace (Ch. 5–6). Demonstrates in Chapter 7 (*'The Transition Today: Scarce Skills, Not Scarce Jobs'*, pp. 101–136) that computer automation replaces *tasks*, not entire occupations: ATM adoption reduced tellers per branch from 20 to 13, but lowered branch operating costs so banks opened 43% more branches, increasing total teller employment while shifting their work to consultative relationship building (source: BOOKS/Innovation_/2015 Learning by Doing - the real connection between_innovation, wages, and wealth.pdf, pp. 7, 71–136).

* **Part III (Chapters 8–14): Technology Policy, Patents, Non-Competes & The Political Economy of Technical Knowledge (pp. 137–228)**:
  * *Against Patent Thickets & Non-Compete Clauses*: Critiques software patent thickets, non-compete agreements (which choked Route 128 in Massachusetts compared to California's open-mobility Silicon Valley), and crony regulatory capture, advocating for open technical standards, community-college apprenticeships, and knowledge-sharing institutions (source: BOOKS/Innovation_/2015 Learning by Doing - the real connection between_innovation, wages, and wealth.pdf, pp. 8, 137–228).
