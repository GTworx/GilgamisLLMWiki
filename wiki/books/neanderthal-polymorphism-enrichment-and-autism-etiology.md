## Summary
Breakthrough paleogenomic and clinical investigation demonstrating a statistically significant enrichment of rare Neanderthal-derived polymorphisms in autistic probands and their non-autistic siblings compared to ethnic controls, integrated with a comprehensive scoping review of autism genetic etiology across underrepresented African populations.

## Analytical Overview
This volume synthesizes pioneering research at the frontier of paleogenomics, evolutionary medicine, and neuropsychiatry. Led by Rini Pauly and colleagues (2024), the primary study investigates whether archaic hominin introgression—resulting from interbreeding between Homo sapiens and Neanderthals in western Eurasia ~50,000 to 60,000 years ago—has shaped contemporary risk architecture for Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD).

Analyzing genomic data from the Simons Simplex Collection (SSC) and SPARK cohorts comprising thousands of autistic probands, unaffected siblings, and matched ancestral controls from the 1000 Genomes Project, the researchers discovered that while common Neanderthal polymorphisms are equally distributed, a specific subset of rare Neanderthal-derived single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) is significantly enriched in individuals with autism and their unaffected siblings. These enriched archaic alleles map preferentially to genes regulating synaptic transmission, neuronal cell adhesion (such as neurexins and neuroligins), and axonal guidance. The findings suggest that certain cognitive, sensory, and behavioral phenotypes categorized under autism today may represent conserved neurological traits selected for specialized environmental adaptation in Pleistocene Eurasia.

To prevent Eurocentric bias, the work pairs these findings with a landmark scoping review of ASD genetic etiology in African populations. Because sub-Saharan African genomes harbor negligible Neanderthal introgression due to lack of ancestral admixture outside Africa, studying African neurodevelopmental genetics provides an invaluable evolutionary control. The review reveals severe diagnostic under-representation, distinct de novo variant spectra, and the critical importance of diversifying global psychiatric genetic datasets to achieve equitable genomic medicine.
