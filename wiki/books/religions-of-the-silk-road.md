## Executive Summary

Written by Iranian and Central Asian religious historian **Richard C. Foltz** (1999, Title Page p. 3, Preface p. 7; 208 pp.; consolidated with the byte-identical duplicate in `Turkiye and Region/`), *Religions of the Silk Road* traces two thousand years of religious diffusion, syncretism, and intellectual cross-pollination along the caravan networks linking the Mediterranean, Iran, India, Central Asia, and China.

Moving briskly through **The Silk Road and Its Travelers** (Ch. 1), **Religion and Trade in Ancient Eurasia** (Ch. 2: Zoroastrianism, Judaism, Sogdian merchant networks), **Buddhism and the Silk Road** (Ch. 3: Kushan patronage and Mahayana's transformation entering China), **A Refuge of Heretics: Nestorians and Manichaeans** (Ch. 4: Uyğur state adoption of Manichaeism and Church of the East missions to Chang'an), **The Islamization of the Silk Road** (Ch. 5: Sufi brotherhoods and Turkic conversion), and the **Mongol Pax** (Ch. 6), Foltz proves that merchants—not conquering armies alone—were history's most decisive missionaries.

---

## Core Historical Insights

1. **Sogdian Merchants as Cultural Translators**: Highlights how Iranian-speaking Sogdian traders operated multi-confessional colonies from Samarkand to Luoyang, transmitting scripts, Buddhist sutras, Manichaean hymns, and Christian liturgies across empires.
2. **Adaptive Syncretism**: Shows how religions survived overland transmission only by absorbing local idioms—producing Greco-Buddhist Gandharan art, Daoist-inflected Chinese Buddhism, and steppe-adapted Central Asian Sufism.
3. **Eurasian Religious Pluralism Before Modernity**: Recovers a cosmopolitan pre-modern world where Nestorian bishops, Tibetan lamas, Muslim jurists, and shamanic khans debated theology side by side at Karakorum and oasis courts.
