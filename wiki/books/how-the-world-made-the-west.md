## Executive Summary

Opening with epigraphs from James Joyce (*"an immense woven fabric"*) and Salman Rushdie (*"Mélange, hotchpotch... is how newness enters the world"*, p. 8), Oxford ancient historian **Josephine Quinn** (Bloomsbury, 2024, Copyright p. 5, Contents pp. 10–11; 577 pp.) dismantles the nineteenth-century "civilizational thinking" myth that "Western Civilization" grew autonomously out of pure Greco-Roman roots. Across thirty brisk, vivid chronological chapters spanning **2000 BCE to 1500 CE**, Quinn demonstrates that **connections—not isolated civilizations—made Europe and the West**.

From Bronze Age Levantine sails, Minoan Crete, Baltic amber routes, and Phoenician **Alphabet City** (Ch. 1–6) through the **Assyrian Mediterranean**, **Gilgamesh** (*He Who Saw the Deep*, Ch. 12), **Persian** imperial inspiration (Ch. 14–15), cosmopolitan **"Rome, Open City"** (Ch. 20), Indian Ocean **Trade Winds** and Saharan **Salt Roads** (Ch. 21–22), the Abbasid **Translation Movement** (Ch. 26), and Sanskrit-Arabic-Latin **Kalila wa-Dimna** networks (Ch. 28), Quinn shows that Europe's Renaissance and global expansion emerged from four millennia of relentless Afro-Eurasian borrowing.

## Core Historical Thesis

1. **Connections Over "Civilizations"**: Replaces Huntington-style sealed cultural blocks with dynamic networks of trade, migration, translation, and hybridity—preferring concrete geography over retroactive ethnic/civilizational labels (Notes to the Reader, p. 12).
2. **Phoenician, Mesopotamian & Persian Foundations of Classical Greece (Ch. 6–16)**: Demonstrates how Greek literacy, coinage, city-state politics, philosophy, and monumental art crystallized through intense entanglement with Phoenician, Anatolian, Assyrian, Egyptian, and Persian neighbors.
3. **Islamic & Afro-Asian Conduits to Modern Europe (Ch. 21–30)**: Traces how Baghdad's Translation Movement, Al-Andalus, Sicilian courts, West African gold routes, and Asian paper/gunpowder/compass technologies forged the intellectual launchpad for 1500 CE.
