## Overview
Authored by British anthropologist Alfred Gell, this foundational 1992 treatise establishes an anthropological theory of visual art grounded in 'technical enchantment'. Gell argues that art objects function not through semiotic meaning, but as technical traps—displays of superhuman artistic virtuosity that captivate, intimidate, and psychologically overpower the spectator through sheer aesthetic technical mastery.

## Key Takeaways
- **Key Finding**: Deconstructs art as an agency-laden technology of psychological captivation and political intimidation.
- **Key Finding**: Defines the 'halo effect' of technical difficulty: viewers revere art because they cannot imagine how it was physically fabricated.
- **Key Finding**: Applies the framework across Trobriand canoe prows, medieval cathedrals, and modern computational spectacles.

## Critical Excerpts & Analysis
> "Art objects are technical weapons designed to enchant; we revere the work of art precisely because we are bewildered by the miraculous technical mastery required to bring it into existence."
