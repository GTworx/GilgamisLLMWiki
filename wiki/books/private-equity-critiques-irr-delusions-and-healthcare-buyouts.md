## Summary
Master investigative, economic, and critical compendium interrogating the real-world performance, social costs, and financial engineering of private equity. Features Oxford Professor Ludovic Phalippou's seminal 'The Delusion of Private Equity IRRs', AFREF's damning study 'Profits Over Patients: The Harmful Effects of Private Equity on the US Healthcare System', Matt Levine's analyses on retail 401(k) inclusion of private equity, and empirical tables detailing PE as the primary beneficiary of Quantitative Easing.

## Analytical Overview
This volume convenes the most rigorous academic and investigative critiques challenging the claims of superior risk-adjusted returns and social efficiency promoted by the private equity industry.

Key critical investigations:
1. **The IRR Delusion & Subscription Credit Lines**: Anchored by Ludovic Phalippou’s landmark Oxford research, demonstrating that reported Internal Rates of Return (IRR) are structurally distorted through the widespread use of short-term subscription lines of credit (capital call facilities). By delaying LP capital calls, GPs artificially shorten the cash flow duration, generating inflated optical IRRs (e.g. 25%) while delivering net multiple on invested capital (MOIC) that merely matches low-cost public equity indices (S&P 500) after compounding astronomical management fees and carried interest.
2. **Healthcare Buyouts & 'Profits Over Patients'**: Compiles comprehensive empirical evidence from CEPR and AFREF documenting how debt-fueled roll-ups of hospitals, physician practices, nursing homes, and emergency staffing agencies lead to staff cuts, increased patient mortality, aggressive surprise billing, and the stripping of valuable hospital real estate via sale-leaseback transactions.
3. **The Push into 401(k)s & QE Windfalls**: Analyzes Matt Levine’s *Money Stuff* commentary and institutional white papers tracking PE's desperate regulatory push to open retail 401(k) retirement accounts to private assets just as zero-interest-rate Quantitative Easing ended, threatening retail savers with illiquid, high-fee assets.
