## Executive Summary

*Russian Politics from Lenin to Putin* (Palgrave Macmillan / St Antony's Series, Oxford, 2010, 240 pp.)—edited by [[stephen-fortescue]] (University of New South Wales) in honor of pioneering Sovietologist **T. H. ("Harry") Rigby**, with chapters by [[sheila-fitzpatrick]], Graeme Gill, Leslie Holmes, [[archie-brown]], Peter Reddaway, and Eugene Huskey—examines the deep structural continuities and ruptures of Russian governance across a full century from Bolshevik state-building to Vladimir Putin's managed authoritarianism (source: BOOKS/Politics_/Russian Politics from Lenin to Putin ( PDFDrive ).pdf, Contents, Contributors & Preface, pp. vii–xiii).

Built around Rigby's famous conceptualization of the Soviet Union as a **"mono-organizational society"** governed by goal-rationality rather than market or legal-rationality, the volume investigates four enduring axes of Russian politics: the perpetual tug-of-war between **institutionalization and personalism**, the inner-circle mechanics of Kremlin rule, shifting modes of regime **legitimation** (from Marxist-Leninist teleology and urban symbolism to post-Soviet performance legitimacy), and elite recruitment through **pantouflage à la russe**—the revolving door binding state security/officialdom directly to major Russian corporations (pp. vii–viii, 1–214).

## Key Arguments & Structural Breakdown

### Chapters 1–3: Rigby's Legacy, Institutionalization vs. Personalism & Stalin's Team (pp. 1–75)
- **T. H. Rigby's Analytical Framework (Ch. 1, Fortescue)**: Synthesizes Rigby's foundational contributions to Sovietology—nomenklatura patronage, *kto kogo*, and the mono-organizational hierarchy—and assesses their relevance to post-Soviet Russia (pp. 1–20).
- **Policy-Making: Institutions vs. Personalism (Ch. 2, Fortescue)**: Compares institutional procedures against personalist patron–client networks from Lenin through Brezhnev, Yeltsin, and Putin, showing that informal personal networks repeatedly subvert formal constitutional rules (pp. 21–50).
- **Stalin and the Inner Circle, 1925–33 (Ch. 3, [[sheila-fitzpatrick]])**: Uses archival correspondence to reveal how Stalin operated initially as a manipulative "team captain" coordinating loyal lieutenants (Molotov, Kaganovich, Ordzhonikidze) before personalist terror shattered collective leadership (pp. 51–75).

### Chapters 4–5: Symbolic Urbanism & Shifting Legitimacy (pp. 76–126)
- **Building the Communist Future (Ch. 4, Graeme Gill)**: Shows how Soviet urban architecture, squares, and monumental planning functioned as a physical instrument of ideological legitimation (pp. 76–100).
- **Legitimacy Revisited (Ch. 5, Leslie Holmes)**: Traces Russia's transition from ideological goal-rational legitimation to eudemonic (economic-performance), nationalist, and legal-procedural claims—exposing how pervasive corruption erodes legitimacy across both Soviet and post-Soviet periods (pp. 101–126).

### Chapters 6–9: Perestroika, Collapse, and Putin's Elite Merger (pp. 127–214)
- **Perestroika as Revolution from Above (Ch. 6, [[archie-brown]])**: Argues that Mikhail Gorbachev's top-down ideational transformation—enabled by the immense institutional authority of the General Secretaryship—was decisive in dismantling Marxist-Leninist orthodoxy peacefully (pp. 127–151).
- **Popular Disaffection & Soviet Collapse (Ch. 7, Peter Reddaway)**: Weighs grassroots nationalist, labor, and civic alienation against elite fragmentation in explaining the sudden 1991 dissolution of the USSR (pp. 152–184).
- ***Pantouflage à la russe* (Ch. 8, Eugene Huskey)**: Empirically maps the careers and educational backgrounds of senior executives across Russia's top 20 corporations (Tables 8.1–8.2, pp. viii, 185–204), documenting how Putin reversed 1990s oligarch capture by embedding state officials and *siloviki* directly atop strategic corporate boards—a state-led fusion of political authority and economic capital (pp. 185–214).
