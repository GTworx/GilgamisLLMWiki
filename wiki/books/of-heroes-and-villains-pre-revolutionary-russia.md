## Executive Summary

Written by German historian of Eastern Europe **Anke Hilbrenner** (De Gruyter Open Access, 2018, 20 pp., p. 1), this incisive historical-sociological book chapter investigates how pre-1917 Russian revolutionary terrorists discursively inverted moral roles—casting their assassins as noble **martyr-heroes** and their murdered targets as **historical perpetrators** who deserved death. Opening with the paradox that Soviet memory never framed Vladimir Lenin as a "terrorist victim" despite Fanny Kaplan's fatal 1918 shooting (pp. 1–2), Hilbrenner centers her case study on the February 1905 assassination of Moscow Governor-General **Grand Duke Sergei Aleksandrovich** by Socialist-Revolutionary (PSR) bomber **Ivan Kalyaev**.

---

## Core Historical & Sociological Findings

1. **Target Selection & "Emotional Communities" (p. 1)**: Demonstrates that the PSR deliberately selected a deeply unpopular royal target—exploiting both political repression and covert whispers regarding the Grand Duke's homosexuality—to forge a silent "emotional community" of relief extending even into tsarist bureaucratic elites.
2. **Narrative Persistence & Counter-Memory**: Shows how Kalyaev's self-staging as a chivalrous martyr (later immortalized by Albert Camus in *The Just Assassins*) dominated historiography, while Grand Duchess **Elizaveta Fedorovna**'s grieving counter-narrative—visiting her husband's killer in prison and founding a convent—challenged the erasure of the victim's humanity.
3. **Heroism vs. Passive Victimhood**: Illuminates how nineteenth-century revolutionary ideology treated violence as "the midwife of history" (p. 2), making passive victimhood incompatible with heroic myth-making.
