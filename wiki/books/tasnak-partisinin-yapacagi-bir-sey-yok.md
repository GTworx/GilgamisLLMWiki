## Executive Summary

Written by **Hovhannes Kajaznuni** (*Ovanes Kaçaznuni*, 1868–1938)—architect, veteran leader of the Armenian Revolutionary Federation (Dashnaktsutyun / Taşnak), and the **first Prime Minister of the First Republic of Armenia (1918–1919)**—and presented here in **Arif Acaloğlu**'s Turkish translation published by Kaynak Yayınları / Istanbul Chamber of Commerce (Jan. 2008, ISBN 975-343-438-3, pp. 2–6; 122 pp.), *The Armenian Revolutionary Federation Has Nothing More to Do* is a celebrated and fiercely debated 1923 political self-critique delivered to the ARF Bucharest Party Conference.

Looking back in bitter disillusionment after the collapse of the 1918–1920 Armenian Republic, the Treaty of Alexandropol/Kars, and Sovietization, Kajaznuni delivers an unflinching postmortem of Dashnak strategic miscalculations during 1914–1920: over-reliance on Tsarist Russia and Western Allied promises (Sèvres), organizing volunteer militias without realistic assessment of Ottoman power, rejecting early compromise, and overestimating diplomatic leverage. Concluding that the party's historical mission had ended, Kajaznuni urged the ARF to dissolve and accept Soviet Armenia as the only viable geopolitical shelter.

## Core Historical & Historiographical Context

1. **Primary-Source Strategic Postmortem (1923)**: Offers candid insider testimony from Armenia's own founding prime minister on Great Power manipulation, wartime volunteer mobilization, and the disastrous consequences of maximalist territorial diplomacy.
2. **Contested Role in Turkish–Armenian Historiography**: Widely cited in Turkish diplomatic and historical publications (as reflected in the 2008 İTO/Kaynak preface, pp. 4–6) to highlight wartime armed insurrection and Entente entanglement, while historians of the Armenian Genocide note that Kajaznuni was diagnosing *political-military blunders* rather than exonerating Ottoman mass deportations—which he himself described as catastrophic tragedy.
3. **Lesson in Small-State Realism**: Endures as a classic case study in the peril of relying on distant Great Power guarantees during imperial wars.
