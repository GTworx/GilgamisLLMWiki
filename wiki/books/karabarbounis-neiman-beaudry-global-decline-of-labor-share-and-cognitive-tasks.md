## Summary
Landmark macroeconomic investigations by Loukas Karabarbounis, Brent Neiman (Chicago Booth), Paul Beaudry, David Green, and Benjamin Sand documenting two profound global structural shifts: 1) The secular decline of labor's share of national income across 59 countries driven by falling capital goods prices; and 2) 'The Great Reversal'—the collapse in growth of demand for cognitive skill and analytical tasks after the year 2000.

## Analytical Overview
### Historical Context & Institutional Setting
For over a century, Bowley's Law and Kaldor's stylized facts held that the share of national income paid to labor was an immutable constant (~65–70%). Karabarbounis and Neiman overturned this dogma in their landmark Quarterly Journal of Economics paper. Concurrently, Beaudry et al. observed that despite surging college graduation rates, cognitive labor demand plateaued following the dot-com crash, forcing college graduates to cascade downward into low-skill service jobs.

### Core Theoretical Foundations & Methodological Architecture
Karabarbounis and Neiman model a CES production function with an elasticity of substitution between capital and labor greater than unity ($\sigma > 1$). Because the relative price of investment and computing goods plunged by over 50% between 1975 and 2012, firms substituted labor with cheap capital, driving down labor share by roughly 5 percentage points globally. Beaudry models the IT revolution through boom-and-maturity cycles: after an initial build-out requiring massive cognitive labor, software infrastructure matured, depressing cognitive employment growth.

### Key Empirical Findings, Case Studies & Frameworks
The empirical data proves that labor share decline is not an artifact of US manufacturing off-shoring: it occurred within 42 of 59 analyzed nations and across virtually all non-tradeable domestic industries. Beaudry documents that after 2000, high-skilled workers increasingly displaced middle-skilled workers in routine jobs, while real wages for college graduates stagnated for two decades.

### Critical Assessment, Methodological Rigor & Strategic Implications
These papers demolished decades of complacent macroeconomic assumptions. By proving that automation can permanently lower labor's slice of national prosperity, their findings sparked global policy debates on capital taxation, sovereign wealth dividends, and universal basic income.
