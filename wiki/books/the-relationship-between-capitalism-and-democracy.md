## Executive Summary

*The Relationship Between Capitalism and Democracy* (Bachelor's Honors Thesis in Philosophy, Politics, Economics, and Law, University of Arizona, May 2017, 34 pp., supervised by philosopher Thomas Christiano) by [[aleksandra-evelyn-petkovic]] delivers a crisp, rigorously argued philosophical synthesis of the central structural paradox of modern liberal societies: while **capitalism provides a necessary minimal condition for democracy**—citizen independence from the state via private property and non-state employment—capitalism simultaneously **prevents democracy from achieving its full normative ideals of political equality and liberty** (source: BOOKS/Politics_/azu_etd_hr_2017_0172_sip1_m.pdf, Abstract & Introduction, pp. 2–7).

Synthesizing Milton Friedman, Robert Dahl, Charles Lindblom, Thomas Christiano, Joshua Cohen, Lawrence Lessig, and Raymond Geuss, Petkovic dissects three escalating mechanisms through which private capital constrains popular self-rule: (1) direct electoral **money as a gatekeeper**, (2) indirect structural veto via the **"privileged position of business"** and **"market as a prison,"** and (3) deep ideological shaping of public discourse ("curved influence" and class-biased consciousness) (pp. 7–27).

## Key Arguments & Structural Breakdown

### Introduction: The Democratic Ideal & Capitalism's Functional Necessity (pp. 2–7)
- **Two Core Democratic Principles**: Grounded in Robert Dahl (*A Preface to Economic Democracy*, 1985), defines full democracy through (1) **Political Equality**—not merely one-person-one-vote, but equal opportunity to influence collective agendas regardless of wealth—and (2) **Liberty**, understood as each adult's autonomy and freedom of thought (pp. 4–5).
- **Why Capitalism Is Necessary**: Endorses Milton Friedman's and Charles Lindblom's empirical observation that no durable democratic nation-state has arisen without a market economy, explaining that private property insulates dissenting citizens from economic retaliation by a monolithic socialist state (pp. 3, 5–6).

### Part I (Direct Power): Money as a Gatekeeper in Campaigns (pp. 7–12)
- **Agenda-Setting by the Affluent**: Applies Thomas Christiano's concept of **money as a gatekeeper** and Lawrence Lessig's dependency critique to show that high campaign costs force viable candidates to court the top 10% income bracket, where business outspends organized labor by an 11:1 margin—systematically marginalizing non-affluent priorities even without crude *quid pro quo* bribery (pp. 8–12).

### Part II (Indirect Power): The Privileged Position & the Market as a Prison (pp. 12–16)
- **Dual Systems of Authority**: Draws on Charles Lindblom (*Politics and Markets*, 1977; *The Market as Prison*, 2003) to argue that corporations are not merely one interest group among many, but a second system of public authority whose investment and employment decisions act as an **"automatic punishing mechanism"** whenever policymakers propose profit-reducing reforms (pp. 12–14).
- **Structural Inducements & Rare "Jailbreaks"**: Shows how anticipating layoffs, inflation, or capital flight leads officials to preemptively offer tax concessions and remove structural reforms from the agenda—with only rare historical crises (such as the New Deal or postwar European reconstruction) permitting temporary "jailbreaks" (pp. 14–15).

### Part III (Deep Power): Unilateral Persuasion & Constraining Freedom of Thought (pp. 17–30)
- **Curved Influence & Class Consciousness**: Combines Lindblom's concept of "lopsided, nearly unilateral persuasion" on core grand issues (private enterprise, corporate autonomy, wealth distribution) with Raymond Geuss's analysis of ideological selection principles, concluding that dominant-class control over media and framing narrows the horizon of open democratic deliberation itself (pp. 17, 25–26).
