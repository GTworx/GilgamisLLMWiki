## Analytical Overview
### The Mathematical History of the 37% Rule

In *Who Solved the Secretary Problem?*, published in *Statistical Science*, Thomas S. Ferguson writes the definitive intellectual and historical biography of one of the most famous problems in probability theory: **The Secretary Problem** (also known as the Marriage Problem, the Sultan's Dowry Problem, or the Best-Choice Problem).

### The Classical Problem and the 1/e Rule

The problem considers selecting the single best candidate out of $n$ applicants interviewed sequentially in random order, with the constraint that decisions must be made immediately and irreversibly after each interview:
- Ferguson derives the classical solution: reject the first $n/e \approx 36.8\%$ of applicants, and then select the very first subsequent candidate who is better than everyone seen previously.
- As $n \to \infty$, the probability of successfully choosing the absolute best applicant converges precisely to **$1/e \approx 0.368$**.

### Tracing the Origins: From Kepler to Dynkin

Ferguson resolves the historical mystery of who actually formulated and solved the problem:
- Johannes Kepler famously deployed an intuitive version of the stopping rule when interviewing eleven potential brides following the death of his first wife.
- Tracing mathematical roots through Arthur Cayley (1875) and Richard Fox (1958), Ferguson proves that the modern mathematical formulation was independently discovered and popularized around 1960 by Eugene Dynkin, Dennis Lindley, and Martin Gardner.
