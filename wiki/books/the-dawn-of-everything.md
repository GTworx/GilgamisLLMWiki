## Executive Summary

Co-authored over a decade by late LSE anthropologist **David Graeber** (1961–2020) and UCL comparative archaeologist **David Wengrow** (2021, Contents pp. 4–5; 736 pp.), *The Dawn of Everything* mounts a monumental archaeological and ethnographic demolition of the twin Enlightenment fables that still dominate popular "Big History": Rousseau's egalitarian "Noble Savage" ruined by agriculture, and Hobbes's violent anarchy tamed only by coercive states.

Across twelve sweeping chapters—beginning with **The Indigenous Critique** (Ch. 2, tracing how Wendat philosopher **Kandiaronk**'s sharp critiques of European inequality and obedience helped ignite the French Enlightenment)—Graeber and Wengrow demonstrate that prehistoric humans were neither politically naive nor trapped in linear evolutionary stages (band → tribe → chiefdom → state). Instead, for tens of thousands of years, societies consciously experimented with **seasonal political alternation** (Ch. 3), **schismogenesis** (neighbors deliberately defining themselves as opposites, such as slave-holding Northwest Coast foragers vs. egalitarian Californians, Ch. 5), **play farming** (Ch. 6–7), and **vast egalitarian cities without kings**—from Trypillia mega-sites in Ukraine and Indus Valley Mohenjo-daro to Teotihuacan and early Mesopotamia (Ch. 8–9).

## Core Analytical Breakthroughs

1. **Three Primordial Human Freedoms (Ch. 3 & 10–12)**: Identifies the baseline liberties that prehistoric peoples routinely guarded—(1) the freedom to move away, (2) the freedom to disobey commands, and (3) the freedom to create or transform social relationships—asking how humanity got "stuck" in rigid hierarchies.
2. **Three Elementary Forms of Domination (Ch. 10: *Why the State Has No Origin*)**: Deconstructs "the State" into three separable powers—control of violence (*sovereignty*), control of information (*bureaucracy*), and individual charisma (*competitive politics*)—showing ancient societies combined only one or two for millennia before modern states fused all three.
3. **Recovering Political Imagination**: Directly challenges Jared Diamond, Steven Pinker, and Yuval Noah Harari's deterministic narratives, arguing that if inequality was never an inevitable byproduct of farming or urban scale, humanity retains far broader institutional freedom today.
