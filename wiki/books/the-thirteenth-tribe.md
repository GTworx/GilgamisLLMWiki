## Executive Summary

Written by Hungarian-British novelist and essayist **Arthur Koestler** (1905–1983; Copyright ©1976, Popular Library/Random House, p. 6; consolidated here across all three library PDF editions: 324 pp., 99 pp., and 85 pp.), *The Thirteenth Tribe* popularized the medieval history of the **Khazar Qağanate**—the powerful Turkic steppe empire spanning the Volga, Don, and North Caucasus (7th–10th centuries CE) whose ruling elite converted to **Judaism** c. 740 CE while balancing geopolitical independence between Christian Byzantium and the Islamic Caliphate.

Structured in two halves plus appendices on the famous **Khazar Correspondence** (Contents, p. 7)—**Part One: Rise and Fall of the Khazars** (Ch. I–IV: *Rise, Conversion, Decline, Fall*) and **Part Two: The Heritage** (Ch. V–VIII: *Exodus, Where From?, Cross-currents, Race and Myth*)—Koestler drew on D. M. Dunlop and A. N. Poliak to advance the controversial **"Khazar Hypothesis"**: that following Khazaria's tenth-to-thirteenth-century collapse, Judaized Khazars migrated westward into Poland and Eastern Europe to form the primary demographic core of Ashkenazi Jewry.

## Core Historical Themes & Critical Assessment

1. **Khazaria as Geopolitical Hinge (Part One)**: Synthesizes Arab (Ibn Fadlan, al-Mas'udi, al-Muqaddasi), Byzantine (Constantine Porphyrogenitus), and Hebrew sources illustrating Khazaria's dual kingship (*Qağan* and *Beg*), commercial control of Volga-Caspian routes, and strategic role halting Umayyad expansion north of the Caucasus.
2. **The "Khazar Hypothesis" & Modern Scientific Consensus (Part Two)**: While Koestler explicitly wrote to undermine racial antisemitism by dissolving myths of biological homogeneity (Ch. VIII), **subsequent historical linguistics (Yiddish origins) and population genetics (2010s–2020s autosomal DNA studies)** have decisively refuted a predominantly Khazar origin for Ashkenazi Jews, showing predominantly Levantine + Southern/Central European ancestry.
3. **Enduring Historiographical Value**: Remains a vivid, widely cited entry point into Khazar studies—best read alongside rigorous Turkological scholarship like Peter Golden's work.
