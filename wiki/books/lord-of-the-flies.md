## Executive Summary

Nobel laureate **William Golding**'s classic 1954 allegorical novel *Lord of the Flies* (12 chapters, Contents p. 4; 251 pp.) strands a group of British schoolboys on an uninhabited Pacific island after a wartime plane crash ("No grownups!", Ch. 1, p. 7) to conduct a harrowing literary thought experiment on leadership, institutional fragility, and the thin veneer of civilization.

What begins as a rational parliamentary assembly convened around the **conch shell** under elected chief **Ralph** and bespectacled rationalist **Piggy** (Ch. 1–2, pp. 6–57) steadily fractures under the charismatic, fear-exploiting rivalry of choir-leader **Jack Merridew**. As hunters abandon the rescue signal fire for bloodlust, face paint, tribal chanting, and scapegoating, the boys' projection of an external "Beast" culminates in the visionary boy **Simon**'s revelation before the severed pig's head (*The Lord of the Flies*, Ch. 8)—that the beast is not an outside monster, but *"only us"*—followed by the ritualized murders of Simon and Piggy and a terrifying island-wide manhunt broken only by a naval officer's ironic arrival (Ch. 12).

---

## Core Political & Leadership Themes

1. **Democratic Order vs. Charismatic Demagoguery**: Pits Ralph's rule-bound, future-oriented stewardship (shelters, signal fire, conch protocol) and Piggy's scientific reason against Jack's short-term gratification, tribal spectacle, and weaponized fear.
2. **Deindividuation & Moral Collapse**: Shows how war paint ("Painted Faces and Long Hair", Ch. 4) liberates boys from shame and conscience, enabling collective cruelty and mob conformity (foreshadowing modern social-psychology studies of conformity and moral disengagement).
3. **Human Nature & Institutional Guardrails**: Rejects naive Victorian optimism (*The Coral Island*) in favor of a sober tragic realism: civilization is not automatic, and without resilient norms, courage, and accountability, fear rapidly converts communities into authoritarian tribes.
