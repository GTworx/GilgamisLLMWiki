## Executive Summary

*Napoleon: A Biography* (originally published 1997; Pimlico / Random House, 1998, 767 pp.) by British historian [[frank-mclynn]] is a sweeping, psychologically acute 28-chapter study of **Napoleon Bonaparte** (1769–1821) that steers deliberately between Bonapartist hero-worship and partisan debunking (source: BOOKS/Biography_/Napoleon_ A Biography ( PDFDrive ).pdf, Cover Blurbing, Copyright & Contents, pp. 2, 21, 23–25).

Tracing Napoleon's Promethean arc from Corsican outsider and Jacobin artillery officer through the Italian and Egyptian campaigns, the Brumaire coup, the Napoleonic Code, imperial coronation (1804), mastery of Europe at Austerlitz, overreach in Spain and Russia (1812), Elba, Waterloo, and final exile on St. Helena, McLynn portrays a towering paradox: simultaneously Enlightenment rationalist and superstitious fatalist, tactical genius and strategic gambler, legal codifier and ruthless autocrat (pp. 2, 27–696).

## Key Arguments & Structural Breakdown

### Corsica, Revolution & Ascent to Power (Chs. 1–12, pp. 29–290)
- **Outsider Ambition**: Examines how Corsican family honor, Jesuit-influenced military schooling, and the chaos of the French Revolution forged Napoleon's ferocious drive, meritocratic ethos, and mastery of mobile artillery warfare from Toulon to Lodi and Rivoli (pp. 29–200).
- **Egypt & 18 Brumaire**: Analyzes the 1798 Egyptian expedition—simultaneously military adventure and scientific-Orientalist enterprise—and Napoleon's swift political seizure of power as First Consul (pp. 201–290).

### Empire, Statecraft & Operational Mastery (Chs. 13–21, pp. 291–521)
- **Dual Legacy of the Consulate & Empire**: Contrasts Napoleon's enduring institutional reforms—the *Code Napoléon*, centralized prefectures, Banque de France, and meritocratic *lycées*—with the dynastic hubris, police censorship, and Continental System blockade that guaranteed perpetual coalition warfare across Europe (pp. 291–521).

### Hubris, Russia, Waterloo & St. Helena (Chs. 22–28, pp. 522–696)
- **Strategic Overextension**: Diagnoses the "Spanish ulcer" and the catastrophic 1812 invasion of Russia as decisive turning points where operational brilliance could no longer rescue unattainable political ends, culminating in Leipzig, the Hundred Days, Waterloo, and Napoleon's self-mythologizing final years on St. Helena (pp. 522–696).
