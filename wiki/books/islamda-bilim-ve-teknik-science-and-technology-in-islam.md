## Executive Summary

Fuat Sezgin's five-volume *İslam'da Bilim ve Teknik* (*Science and Technology in Islam*, created in collaboration with Eckhard Neubauer at the Institute for the History of Arabic-Islamic Science in Frankfurt am Main) is one of the most ambitious material-history reconstructions of medieval science ever produced. Combining deep archival historiography with an illustrated catalogue of over 800 physical replicas built from surviving Arabic, Persian, and Ottoman manuscripts, Sezgin challenges the entrenched Eurocentric narrative that modern science leaped directly from ancient Greece across a thousand-year "Dark Ages" void into the European Renaissance (Cilt I, p. 9).

Instead, Sezgin documents an uninterrupted, cumulative bridge spanning the 8th through 16th centuries—from Baghdad, Damascus, and Córdoba to Samarkand (Uluğ Bey) and Istanbul (Fatih Sultan Mehmed, Cilt I, p. 7)—showing how Islamic scientists inherited Greek, Indian, and Persian foundations, elevated experimental and mathematical instrumentation, and transmitted decisive technical breakthroughs to Europe.

## Key Historiographical Contributions

1. **Science as Universal Cumulative Heritage**: Sezgin argues in his opening preface that scientific progress cannot be monopolized by any single geography or civilization; it represents an unbroken chain of cross-cultural inquiry (Cilt I, p. 5).
2. **Material Reconstruction as Proof**: Rather than relying solely on textual claims, the Frankfurt Institute (and later Istanbul's Museum of the History of Science and Technology in Islam in Gülhane Park) engineered working instruments directly from manuscript diagrams, proving the mechanical precision of medieval Islamic astrolabes, water clocks, surgical tools, and automata.
3. **Cartographic & Observational Milestones**: Volumes II and III highlight how Islamic trigonometric geography and mathematical map-projection laid crucial groundwork for Renaissance European portolan charts and world maps.
