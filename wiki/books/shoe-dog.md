## Executive Summary

Written by Nike co-founder and longtime CEO/chairman **Phil Knight** (Scribner, 2016, copyright p. 369), *Shoe Dog* is one of modern business literature's most candid, literary entrepreneurial memoirs. Opening with Shunryu Suzuki's Zen epigraph on *Beginner's Mind* (p. 5) and a foggy 1962 morning run in Oregon (pp. 6–8), Knight chronicles the chaotic 18-year chronicle (1962–1980, TOC p. 3) that turned a $50 loan from his skeptical father and a Stanford MBA paper about importing Japanese running shoes into **Blue Ribbon Sports** and eventually **Nike**.

Rather than a polished retrospective of inevitable triumph, *Shoe Dog* reads like a breathless survival thriller: Knight spent nearly two decades teetering on the brink of insolvency, juggling angry bankers, customs lawsuits ($25 million U.S. government ASP tariff threat), betrayal by supplier Onitsuka Tiger, factory scrambles in Japan and Asia, and personal sacrifice before Nike's December 1980 IPO finally secured financial survival (pp. 326–364).

## Core Entrepreneurial Lessons

1. **The "Crazy Idea" & Direct Conviction**: Knight could never sell encyclopedias or mutual funds, yet sold Japanese Tiger shoes effortlessly out of the trunk of his Plymouth Valiant because he genuinely believed in running—demonstrating that authentic product obsession outperforms slick salesmanship.
2. **Product Innovation & Co-Founder Complementarity**: Pairs Knight's relentless global sourcing and risk appetite with legendary University of Oregon track coach **Bill Bowerman**'s eccentric prototyping (culminating in pouring urethane into a household waffle iron to invent the Waffle sole).
3. **Assembling a Misfit Band of Missionaries ("The Buttfaces")**: Celebrates the fiercely loyal, unconventional early team (Jeff Johnson, Bob Woodell, Rob Strasser, Del Hayes) who bonded through chaos, blunt debate, and shared mission.
4. **The Cash-Flow Razor's Edge**: Vividly illustrates the paradox of high-growth physical-goods entrepreneurship—the faster Blue Ribbon/Nike doubled sales every year, the closer working-capital demands pushed it toward bankruptcy until Nissho Iwai trading credit and IPO equity unlocked breathing room.
