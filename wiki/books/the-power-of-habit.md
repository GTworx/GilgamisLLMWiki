## Executive Summary

Pulitzer Prize–winning investigative journalist **Charles Duhigg** (Random House, 2012, Copyright p. 2; 295 pp.) synthesizes MIT basal-ganglia neuroscience, consumer marketing history, organizational turnarounds, and social-movement sociology into a unified framework of automatic behavior. Across three escalating scales—**Part One: The Habits of Individuals** (Ch. 1–3), **Part Two: The Habits of Successful Organizations** (Ch. 4–7), and **Part Three: The Habits of Societies** (Ch. 8–9)—Duhigg shows that over 40% of daily actions stem not from conscious deliberation but from deeply encoded neural loops.

At the core of the book lies the three-step **Habit Loop** (**Cue → Routine → Reward**, powered by an anticipatory **Craving**, Ch. 1–2) and the **Golden Rule of Habit Change** (Ch. 3): you cannot extinguish an old habit; you can only *keep the same cue and reward while inserting a new routine*, anchored by belief and community.

## Core Frameworks & Insights

1. **The Habit Loop & Craving Engine (Ch. 1–2)**: Traces how Claude Hopkins's Pepsodent campaign ("film on teeth" + minty tingle) and Procter & Gamble's Febreze turnaround succeeded only after identifying a sensory craving that locked the cue-routine-reward loop in place.
2. **Keystone Habits & Organizational Crisis Design (Ch. 4–6)**: Profiles Alcoa CEO Paul O'Neill's obsession with worker safety as a **Keystone Habit** that triggered cascading improvements in quality, communication, and profits; Starbucks's LATTE willpower scripts; and how leaders like Rhode Island Hospital and London Underground reformers leverage crises to rewrite toxic institutional routines.
3. **Predictive Analytics & Social Movements (Ch. 7–9)**: Examines Target's pregnancy-prediction modeling (Ch. 7), the Montgomery Bus Boycott's fusion of strong ties, weak ties, and new civic habits (Ch. 8), and the moral/legal question of free will over ingrained habits (Ch. 9).
