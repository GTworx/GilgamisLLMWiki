## Executive Summary

*Molla Nasreddin: The Magazine That Would've, Could've, Should've* (Christoph Keller Editions / JRP|Ringier, 2011, 107 pp. monograph selection) is curated and introduced by the Eurasian art and research collective [[slavs-and-tatars]], bringing into English translation the pioneering early-twentieth-century Azeri satirical weekly *Molla Nasreddin* (published 1906–1930 across Tbilisi, Tabriz, and Baku) founded and edited by [[jalil-mammadguluzadeh]] (1866–1932) (source: BOOKS/Politics_/molla_nasreddin_slavs_and_tatars.pdf, Title Page & Introduction, pp. 2–13).

Named after the iconic medieval Eurasian Sufi wise-fool, *Molla Nasreddin* achieved an extraordinary pre-broadcast reach from Morocco to India by pairing biting, Daumier-like color caricatures—accessible even to illiterate readers—with colloquial Azeri Turkish prose rather than imperial Russian (p. 4). Across twelve thematic sections (*East vs. West, Class, Women, Colonialism, The Caucasus, The Ottoman Empire, Iran, The Balkans, Reform, Islam, Education, Press*), the weekly ruthlessly lampooned clerical hypocrisy, patriarchal double standards, superstitious fatalism, Western imperialism, and local elite "self-colonization," while advancing the **Jadid** reform movement that helped culminate in the 1918–1920 Azerbaijan Democratic Republic—the first Muslim-majority nation to grant women the right to vote (pp. 4–13, 98).

## Key Arguments & Structural Breakdown

### Introduction: The Polyglot Press of Transcaucasia (pp. 4–13)
- **Visual Mass Media & Vernacular Coup**: Explains how devoting half of each eight-page issue to vivid color lithographs and publishing in vernacular Azeri Turkish broke elite linguistic monopolies and reached a 5,000-copy circulation across Eurasia (p. 4).
- **Tbilisi, Tabriz & Baku Trajectory**: Traces the periodical's itinerant history from cosmopolitan Tsarist Tbilisi ("the warm Siberia," 1906–1917), through a brief, turbulent 1921 year in Iranian Azerbaijan (Tabriz), to its co-optation under Soviet Bolshevik directives in Baku during the 1920s (pp. 6, 10).
- **Three Alphabets in Seventy Years**: Highlights the geopolitical turbulence written directly into the Azeri script—shifting from Arabic to Latin (1929), Latin to Cyrillic (1939), and back to Latin (1991)—while reflecting critically on how post-Soviet consumerist modernization complicated the magazine's original faith in Westernization (pp. 10–14, 98).

### Thematic Caricature Archive (12 Sections, pp. 12–107)
- **East vs. West & Elite Hypocrisy (pp. 12–29)**: Features iconic visual satires contrasting modern European infrastructure with stagnant fatalism, mocking double standards of educated Muslim men who veil their wives in the Caucasus while dressing them in European fashion in Paris, and critiquing both the European who "thinks without speaking" and the local pundit who "speaks without thinking" (pp. 14–29).
- **Women's Emancipation & Jadid Education**: Champions girls' schooling, modern curricula, and legal equality against entrenched clerical and patriarchal resistance, connecting satirical journalism directly to early-20th-century constitutionalism in the Caucasus and Iran (pp. 38–63, 98).
- **Anti-Colonialism, Class & Press Freedom**: Exposes Tsarist, British, and European imperial interference across the Ottoman Empire, Persia, and the Balkans alongside the venality of Baku oil barons and local notables—noting the recurring personal hazards faced by Mammadguluzadeh and his illustrators when testing the boundaries of free expression (pp. 6, 30–37, 64–107).

## Key Quotes & Page Citations

- > "Molla Nasreddin managed to do in a pre-capitalist world what today's media titans, in an uncertain, post-capitalist world, can only dream of: speak to the intelligentsia as well as the masses." (source: BOOKS/Politics_/molla_nasreddin_slavs_and_tatars.pdf, Introduction, p. 4)
- > "If self-colonisation features as one of MN's many bêtes noires, the choice to publish in Azeri Turk and not Russian or even Istanbulli (Turkish) provided the best opportunity to slay the beast." (source: BOOKS/Politics_/molla_nasreddin_slavs_and_tatars.pdf, Introduction, p. 98 / PDF p. 5)
- > "Like the best cultural productions, Molla Nasreddin is polyphonic, joyfully self-contradictory and staunchly in favour of the creolisation that results from multiple languages, ideas and identities." (source: BOOKS/Politics_/molla_nasreddin_slavs_and_tatars.pdf, Introduction, p. 11 / PDF p. 6)
