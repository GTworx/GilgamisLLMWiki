# Theme: Nuclear Strategy and Strategic Stability

**Summary**: The doctrines, operational postures, arms control frameworks, and technological dynamics governing the role of nuclear weapons in preventing major power war while avoiding catastrophic escalation.  
**Last updated**: 2026-08-23  

---

## Synthesis

Nuclear deterrence represents the most consequential application of strategic theory in human history. Defined by the existential condition of Mutual Assured Destruction (MAD), nuclear deterrence posits that no rational actor will initiate a major war against a nuclear-armed opponent possessing an invulnerable second-strike capability, as the retaliatory punishment would guarantee unacceptable societal destruction (source: raw/Deterrence in 21st Century Nuclear NetherlandsAnnualReviewO.pdf, ch. 1, pp. 1–6; ch. 5, pp. 65–86).

### Key Dilemmas and Contemporary Challenges
In *Deterrence in the 21st Century*, contributors explore how the 21st-century nuclear landscape is fracturing:
1. **The Erosion of Strategic Stability**: Alexey Arbatov (ch. 5, pp. 65–86) analyzes the collapse of the bilateral arms control architecture (the termination of the ABM Treaty, INF Treaty, Open Skies, and suspension of New START). He details how counterforce precision strike weapons, hypersonic glide vehicles, and national missile defenses threaten the survivability of second-strike retaliatory forces, creating dangerous "use-them-or-lose-them" pressures during a crisis.
2. **Extended Deterrence & Credibility**: Paul van Hooft (ch. 6, pp. 87–107) deconstructs the structural dilemma of US extended nuclear deterrence in Europe and the Indo-Pacific. When adversaries achieve intercontinental nuclear parity with the US homeland, extended deterrence guarantees face skepticism: would the US truly sacrifice Chicago or Los Angeles to defend Tallinn or Tokyo?
3. **The Stability-Instability Paradox**: Explored in South Asia by Sander Ruben Aarten (ch. 12, pp. 215–230), strategic nuclear parity between India and Pakistan induces sub-conventional proxy warfare and lower-level militarized crises, because both actors calculate that full-scale nuclear escalation will be avoided.
4. **Algorithmic C2 and Compressed Time**: Roy Lindelauf (ch. 22, pp. 421–435) analyzes game-theoretic models of nuclear exchange in an era of automated early warning, AI-assisted decision aids, and extreme speed, demonstrating how algorithmic processing can induce unintended catalytic nuclear escalation.
