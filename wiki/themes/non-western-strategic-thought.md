# Theme: Non-Western Strategic Thought

**Summary**: The doctrines, cultural concepts, and operational paradigms of deterrence, coercion, and strategy developed in non-Western strategic cultures, specifically Russia, China, Iran, and South Asia.  
**Last updated**: 2026-08-23  

---

## Synthesis

Western deterrence theory has historically been grounded in Anglo-American rational choice models, viewing deterrence as a binary, peacetime state designed to preserve the status quo. However, as demonstrated across Part II of *Deterrence in the 21st Century*, non-Western strategic cultures operate with radically different conceptual frameworks, vocabulary, and operational assumptions (source: raw/Deterrence in 21st Century Nuclear NetherlandsAnnualReviewO.pdf, ch. 9–13, pp. 161–261; ch. 26, pp. 517–520).

### Key Non-Western Paradigms
1. **Russian *Sderzhivanie* (Dmitry Adamsky, ch. 9, pp. 161–176)**:
   * *Sderzhivanie* translates simultaneously as "containing," "restraining," and "deterring." 
   * Unlike the Western binary separation of war and peace, *sderzhivanie* is conceptualized as a continuous, holistic, and active process across peacetime, crisis, and war.
   * It seamlessly integrates non-military instruments (cyber, information confrontation, diplomatic pressure, economic leverage), strategic deception (*maskirovka*), reflexive control, conventional long-range precision strikes, and non-strategic nuclear signaling to shape adversary perceptions and compel restraint.
2. **Chinese *Weishe* (Dean Cheng, ch. 10, pp. 177–199)**:
   * *Weishe* translates as "coercion," "awe," or "intimidation."
   * In PLA doctrine, *weishe* is not merely passive defense to maintain the status quo; it is an active, proactive instrument used to compel an opponent to submit to Chinese will without fighting (Sun Tzu's ideal of "subduing the enemy without fighting").
   * It operationalizes the "Three Warfares" (*san zhan*): psychological warfare, public opinion/media warfare, and legal warfare (*falüzhan*), integrated with space, cyber, and electronic warfare capabilities.
3. **Iranian Asymmetric Forward Deterrence (Hassan Ahmadian & Payam Mohseni, ch. 13, pp. 231–261)**:
   * Tehran's strategy evolved from defensive balancing to a forward, networked deterrence posture in Syria and the Levant.
   * Leverages non-state regional allies ("Axis of Resistance"), drone and ballistic missile networks, and geographical depth to deter direct Israeli and American conventional attacks.
4. **South Asian Nuclear Dynamics (Sander Ruben Aarten, ch. 12, pp. 215–230)**:
   * Examines how Pakistan's doctrine of Full Spectrum Deterrence (deploying tactical nuclear weapons like Nasr) interacts with India's Cold Start conventional doctrine, illustrating how localized regional rivalries modify classical deterrence assumptions.
