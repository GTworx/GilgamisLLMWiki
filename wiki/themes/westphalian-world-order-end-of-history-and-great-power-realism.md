# Theme: Westphalian World Order, The End of History, and Great-Power Realism

**Summary**: The central grand-strategy debate of modern international relations: whether history converges toward universal liberal democracy or remains governed by competing civilizational visions, sovereignty, and power equilibrium.
**Last updated**: 2026-11-24

---

## Synthesis
**Westphalian World Order, The End of History, and Great-Power Realism** frames the defining post-Cold War contest over how global politics actually works.

On the universalist-liberal pole, [[francis-fukuyama]]'s [[the-end-of-history-and-the-last-man]] argues that modern science and the Hegelian struggle for recognition (*thymos*) drive societies toward liberal democracy—even while warning of the Nietzschean 'last man.' Countering universalist teleology, [[henry-kissinger]]'s [[world-order]] insists no single global order has ever existed, tracing how Westphalian equilibrium collides with Islamic, Chinese, Russian, and American conceptions of legitimacy. Real-world tests abound across the corpus: [[stephen-fortescue]], [[archie-brown]], and [[sheila-fitzpatrick]]'s [[russian-politics-from-lenin-to-putin]] explains how personalism and state–business fusion resurfaced in post-Soviet Russia; [[john-j-mearsheimer]] & [[stephen-m-walt]]'s [[the-israel-lobby-and-us-foreign-policy]] applies structural realism to U.S. Middle East commitments; [[john-bolton]]'s [[the-room-where-it-happened]] champions unilateral American sovereignty; and [[turkic-world-policy-analysts]]'s [[turkic-world-senate-analysis-2026]] captures contemporary great-power competition over Central Asian critical minerals versus Uyghur human-rights accountability.
